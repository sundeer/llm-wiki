# Liberal Neutrality Critique

**Q:** A lot of the New Right's claims about the failure of liberalism have to do with liberalism's supposed claim that it's a neutral arbiter of both morality and spirituality. Is that true? Does liberalism make those claims? Expand on this with concrete examples.

---

## Does Liberalism Claim Moral and Spiritual Neutrality?

The short answer: **yes, partially — and the postliberal critique has genuine intellectual force here, though it is also significantly overstated.**

---

### What Liberalism Actually Claims

Liberal political theory does make a specific, bounded neutrality claim — but it is more careful and more limited than the New Right typically represents. The dominant modern formulation is John Rawls's *political liberalism*: the state should not endorse or impose any "comprehensive doctrine" (any complete account of the good life, whether religious or secular). Instead, it provides a framework of fair procedures and basic rights within which citizens can pursue their own conceptions of the good. "Public reason" — the requirement that political arguments appeal to principles all citizens could accept regardless of their religion or metaphysics — is the procedural expression of this.

This is **not** a claim that morality doesn't matter. Liberalism is explicitly committed to thick moral values: equal human dignity, individual rights, religious toleration, rule of law, democratic legitimacy. What it claims to be neutral *about* is the question of which comprehensive theory of the good (Christianity, Islam, secular humanism, etc.) should be officially enforced by state power.

---

### Where the Postliberal Critique Has Real Force

[[Patrick Deneen]] and [[Adrian Vermeule]] are right that this neutrality claim is partially a fiction — and [[Laura K. Field|Field]] herself concedes the point: "Deneen proves beyond a reasonable doubt that liberalism claims to eschew comprehensive substantive theories of the good, yet inevitably embeds and enforces just such a comprehensive substantive theory."

The argument goes: liberal "procedural neutrality" presupposes and enforces a substantive vision of the good person — autonomous, critically self-determining, freed from inherited tradition and authority. This is not neutral between, say, a devout Catholic and a secular individualist. It systematically privileges the latter. From the [[Postliberalism]] page:

> Liberalism's claim to neutrality is false — it enforces a comprehensive (hedonistic, individualist) theory of the good.

And Deneen frames liberalism as a "fighting faith" in its own right — a world religion with a soteriology, a clergy, sacraments, and an eschatology — that enforces a comprehensive doctrine *while claiming not to.*

**Concrete examples where liberal "neutrality" encodes substantive values:**

1. **Same-sex marriage** (*Obergefell*, 2015): The liberal framing was that the state must be neutral between conceptions of marriage — which means it cannot privilege the natural-law or religious conception over the individual-autonomy one. But choosing "individual autonomy + equal dignity" *over* "community tradition + natural law" is a substantive moral choice, not a neutral one. The liberal state is not refereeing; it is picking a side.

2. **Abortion**: Pre-*Dobbs*, the Roe/Casey framework embedded a substantive theory of bodily autonomy as overriding the competing moral claim about fetal life. Calling this "neutral" about when life begins was sleight of hand: the constitutional structure itself operationalized one moral framework at the expense of another.

3. **Religious expression in public spaces**: The church-state separation principle means a municipality that allows any display in a public square must allow a Satanist one — or none at all. From the perspective of a devout Christian, this isn't neutrality; it's a substantive state determination that Christianity has no privileged civic standing. The liberal answer — "we treat all religions equally" — is itself a thick moral commitment about the relationship between religion and civic life.

4. **Education**: [[Why Liberalism Failed]] (Deneen) argues that liberal universities, committed to "critical individualism" and liberation from inherited tradition, produce graduates who know how to critique everything and affirm nothing. The *ideal of critical autonomy* — the view that the well-formed person is one who has examined and chosen their commitments rather than simply received them — is not neutral between a Thomistic account of education and a Millian one. It privileges the latter.

5. **[[Common Good Constitutionalism]] and originalism's failure**: [[Adrian Vermeule]]'s specific legal complaint is that originalism — the conservative version of "neutral" constitutional interpretation — has systematically failed conservatives because ostensibly neutral methods encode liberal presuppositions. *Bostock v. Clayton County* (2020), where Neil Gorsuch used "originalist" textualism to protect LGBTQ employees from discrimination, seemed to confirm this for the postliberal camp.

---

### Where the New Right Overstates or Distorts the Critique

**1. Conflating limited with unlimited neutrality claims.** Sophisticated liberalism (Rawls) does *not* claim neutrality about basic political morality — it's explicitly committed to equal dignity and basic rights. The New Right is right that procedural liberalism slides into comprehensive liberalism in practice, but wrong to imply liberalism *claims* neutrality about all moral questions.

**2. "Spiritual neutrality" is misframed.** Liberalism's church-state separation is not a claim that spiritual life doesn't matter — it's a claim that the *state* shouldn't adjudicate between competing spiritual traditions. The postliberal response (the state should actively promote Catholic natural law, or a Christian public order) simply replaces a hidden imposition with an explicit one. In a genuinely pluralist society, this creates a different but equally serious problem: *whose* comprehensive doctrine?

**3. The equality charge is confused.** The New Right often bundles "equality" into the neutrality critique — suggesting liberalism falsely claims to be neutral about equality. But liberalism never claimed that; its equality commitments are positive moral claims, not neutral ones.

**4. The alternative is not obviously better on its own terms.** [[Common Good Constitutionalism]] claims to recover an objective, tradition-grounded account of the good. But as Field notes, "the common good" turns out to be as flexible a concept as any Schmittian could hope for — with no clear mechanism for constraining who defines it or how. If liberalism's problem is secretly encoding a comprehensive doctrine, postliberalism's answer is to explicitly encode one, with fewer procedural safeguards against abuse.

---

### The Wiki's Own Judgment

Field's framing is the most precise: Deneen's **diagnosis is correct** but his **prescriptions are dangerous**. The critique of liberal neutrality is philosophically serious and partially valid. Liberalism does smuggle in substantive values under the cover of procedural language. Where the New Right weaponizes this critique is in the leap from "liberalism has a hidden comprehensive doctrine" to "therefore we should replace liberal procedures with an overtly substantive state directed by natural law and Catholic social teaching" — without acknowledging that this "solution" creates the same problem without any of the liberal protections.

The most honest liberal response is not to defend the neutrality claim, but to admit it is always provisional and contested, and to argue that liberal procedures — imperfect as they are — do a better job of managing disagreement among people with radically different comprehensive doctrines than any alternative on offer from the [[Postliberalism|postliberals]].
