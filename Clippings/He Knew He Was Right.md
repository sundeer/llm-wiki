In the spring of 1964, Barry Goldwater, rolling through the primaries en route to the Republican Presidential nomination, paid a visit to the state of Georgia, where some cracks appeared to be developing in a block of delegates his campaign had thought was locked up. An excited band of supporters was on hand to greet him. One of them was selling, from the back of a truck, a soft drink he had concocted called Gold Water—"The Right Drink for the Conservative Taste." The candidate was invited to take a sip. He spat it out. "This tastes like piss!" he said. Goldwater was uncommonly quick on the trigger, and, when he shot, he shot straight. Neither quality is especially conducive to a successful career in American politics.

That fall, Goldwater was on the wrong end of the most overwhelming victory in the history of American Presidential elections. Lyndon Baines Johnson won sixty-one per cent of the popular vote in 1964—still a record. Twenty-seven million Americans marked their ballots for Goldwater, but a subsequent poll indicated that only six million of those voters were actually pro-Goldwater. The rest identified themselves simply as Republican Party loyalists. The consensus of many commentators looking back on the election was that Goldwater was a fluke, a fringe candidate who had stolen the nomination while the rest of his party was napping and had been duly repudiated by the electorate. The consensus of many historians looking back on the nineteen-sixties was that Goldwater was an anachronism, a cowboy who rode in from the wrong decade—the personification of everything the culture couldn't seem to turn its back on fast enough. Books about the nineteen-sixties tended to be written from the point of view of the New Left or the civil-rights movement or the Kennedy brothers. They were not written from the point of view of what editorialists called "little old ladies in tennis shoes," their stereotype of the Goldwater voter. The point of Rick Perlstein's animated re-creation of the Goldwater campaign, "Before the Storm: Barry Goldwater and the Unmaking of the American Consensus" (Hill & Wang; $30), is that Barry Goldwater was as much a man of the nineteen-sixties as Abbie Hoffman or Malcolm X, and, what's more, his shadow looms a good deal larger than theirs. It's not only that more politicians today sound like Goldwater than like Tom Hayden. More politicians today sound like Goldwater than like Lyndon Johnson.

The argument that the Goldwater campaign was the start of something big rather than the last gasp of something small is possibly a little less controversial than Perlstein suggests. Goldwater was not entirely a lone gunslinger. He plainly had a constituency, loath as he was to stroke it, and even in 1964 people could read the signs. That constituency was not little old ladies in tennis shoes, and it wasn't mad bombers, either. It was working-class white men in revolt against integration—the famous "backlash" voters. "The white backlash," the British journalist Godfrey Hodgson wrote in the summer of 1964, "fits into a broader pattern of social and political change that may rewrite the most basic assumptions on which the dominance of the Democrats had been based since 1933." Perlstein, a generation later, says much the same thing.

What he has to add to the story is really the first part of his claim, the idea that Goldwater was part of the nineteen-sixties rebellion. He points out that "The Conscience of a Conservative," Goldwater's ghostwritten manifesto, published in 1960, was snapped off the shelves in college bookstores right along with "The Catcher in the Rye." Young people in the early nineteensixties were in revolt before they had any clear idea what form revolution might take. "Politically, the generation was up for grabs," Perlstein thinks. First, it veered left, but in the end it went right, and much of the country went with it. "Before the Storm" is a history of the early nineteen-sixties in which John Wayne, William F. Buckley, and Young Americans for Freedom take roles usually assigned to Allen Ginsberg, Herbert Marcuse, and Students for a Democratic Society—true prophets of the new dawn. That Perlstein is committed to giving Goldwater and the Goldwaterites their due despite a distaste for their views lends his narrative a certain zest. "Before the Storm" is smart and lively, and the description is delightfully thick. No one would call the narrative streamlined, and the book is badly in need of an epilogue; but the author has a good time with his story, and so does the reader.

Goldwater was an enemy of the establishment. That term was coined by another eminent British journalist who covered American politics, Henry Fairlie. Fairlie first used it to refer to the network of power brokers in English life, from the Archbishop of Canterbury to the editor of the *T.L.S*. In America, it came to refer to the suits—the Washington lawyers, the New York bankers, and the university presidents, along with their academic and journalistic epigones—upon whose wisdom elected officials were accustomed to rely, and without whose approbation no public policy could be expected to succeed. The great virtue of this group from the late nineteen-forties through the early nineteen-sixties was also its prime vulnerability: it believed itself to be above partisanship and self-interest. Its watchword was "consensus," and its instrument was the federal bureaucracy, whose vast resources it undertook to direct in a pragmatic manner conducive to the welfare of all. The establishment was liberal in the sense that it believed in the power of government to solve social problems and improve the quality of life. But it was deeply vested in the status quo as well. Its members had no inclination to question the assumptions of a social and economic dispensation of which they were the most privileged products.

Goldwater's hatred of the establishment and its workings was, at its deepest level, not very different from the New Left's and the counterculture's: he believed that top-down managerial liberalism was a threat to individual autonomy, and he despised the notion that ideology didn't matter. He would not have put it quite this way, but he was haunted by the same spectre that has haunted many other critics of the liberal welfare state: the spectre of soul death. Like Holden Caulfield, Mario Savio, Bob Dylan, Gloria Steinem, Malcolm X, Randle McMurphy, Captain America and Billy—like almost any iconic figure of the era you can name—Goldwater just didn't like being told what to do.

It's one of the reasons he was such a terrible candidate. The more he alienated his audiences, the more certain he became that he was on the right track. He had no great personal ambition to be President; he saw himself as the spokesman for a set of principles, and he had little interest in bending them to suit the wishes of even his admirers. If it tasted like piss, he said so. One of his methods of dealing with chants of "We Want Barry" when he stepped to the microphone was to growl, "If you'll shut up, you'll get him." During his Presidential campaign, he delivered speeches in Memphis and Raleigh advocating the elimination of federal cotton subsidies; in Knoxville, he proposed selling the Tennessee Valley Authority's steam-generating plants; in West Virginia, he denounced the war on poverty; in Fort Worth, he criticized the award of a major defense contract to General Dynamics, and Fort Worth happens to be the city where the military-aircraft division of General Dynamics is. His solution to paternalistic liberalism was also Stokely Carmichael's and John Lennon's. "We will place power back to the people," he said.

To this extent, Perlstein is exactly right: the Goldwater movement was not an undertow. It was part of the wave. "Extremism in the defense of liberty is no vice," Goldwater declared in his acceptance speech at the Republican National Convention. "Moderation in the pursuit of justice is no virtue." They were lines that, against the pleas of half his staff, he insisted on keeping in the speech (he underlined them twice in his copy), and they are generally considered to be the lines that sealed his political doom. As Perlstein points out, though, taking extreme measures on behalf of liberty and justice was the very spirit of the age. The only people who recoiled from the idea were the suits.

Like many historians, going all the way back to Theodore H. White in "The Making of the President 1964," Perlstein thinks that John F. Kennedy's assassination made the Presidency virtually unwinnable for any Republican in 1964. It was, as he says, a "martyr-besotted electorate," and Lyndon Johnson devoted all his formidable political talents to maintaining it in that state of morbid inebriation. Johnson had been nearly as unwelcome in Camelot as Richard Nixon, but he did his utmost not to let it be forgot. He ran as the heir of a man whom he did not particularly like and whose policies he had had, as Vice-President, almost nothing to do with. He rode a dead man's coattails into the White House.

Still, although all successful candidacies are, in the end, the same—they reflect "the will of the people," after all—every failed candidacy fails in its own way, and in the case of Goldwater's there were two major wounds. The first was self-inflicted. Goldwater's inclination to shoot straight did not waver even when he was aiming at his own foot. "You know, I haven't got a really first-class brain," he once told the columnist Stewart Alsop, who obligingly printed the remark (and Alsop was a Republican). Perlstein doesn't mention that one, but there were plenty more Goldwater moments. Goldwater advocated, or uttered words giving the clear appearance that he advocated, using atomic weapons to defoliate the jungle covering the Ho Chi Minh Trail, giving *NATO* commanders discretion to use nuclear bombs as they saw fit, and making Social Security voluntary—a fairly far-out position to take at a time when the New Deal generation was still the dominant voting block. He proved so gifted at exposing his most alarming instincts that it was practically unnecessary for his opponent to attack him.

His opponent attacked him anyway. The other brilliant thing Johnson did as a campaigner, after making certain that voters identified him with his predecessor, was to turn the 1964 race into a referendum on nuclear policy. This was the purpose of his campaign's infamous daisy commercial, in which the image of a little girl pulling the petals off a daisy fades into the sound of a countdown and explosion and the image of a mushroom cloud, with the admonition to vote for Johnson on November 3rd: "The stakes are too high for you to stay home." The spot ran only once as a paid commercial—even Johnson found it excessive—but it was replayed so often on network news programs that something like forty million people saw it. This was the wound that did Goldwater in.

The commercial certainly had a point. Part of the reason Goldwater despised the notion that ideology doesn't matter was that he thought it made liberals soft on Communism. He took ideology to be the whole point of the Cold War, and there is no question of his militancy on the subject. He was one of nineteen senators to vote against the Test Ban Treaty. And, as Perlstein documents, he went on sounding like General Jack D. Ripper even after "Dr. Strangelove" came out, right in the middle of the New Hampshire primary. Anti-Communism was also undoubtedly the leading sentiment among many of the conservative groups, such as the John Birch Society—its leader, the candy manufacturer Robert Welch, believed that Eisenhower was a Communist agent—whose agitation helped seed the ground for the Goldwater insurgency. The decision to campaign against Goldwater as a dangerous warmonger seems logical today, but that is largely because of the effectiveness of the campaign. Goldwater is remembered as a man who proposed using nuclear weapons in Vietnam because that is how Lyndon Johnson wished him to be remembered. Most Americans who voted for Goldwater, though, did not do so because of his position on Vietnam and the worldwide Communist conspiracy. They voted for him because of his position on an issue that Johnson wanted to keep as far from the center of the campaign as he decently could. This was the issue of race.

There are (retrospectively, anyway) comic elements in the story of the Goldwater campaign, but at the heart of it is something tragic. The Senate voted on the Civil Rights Act on June 19, 1964. It was one of the most intense and closely watched congressional actions of the decade, and the most widely publicized vote of Goldwater's Senate career: he had just become the likely Republican Presidential nominee by winning the California primary, less than three weeks before. He voted against the bill. Two weeks later, Lyndon Johnson (who had pushed the legislation, he maintained, as a tribute to the memory of John F. Kennedy) signed it into law.

Most of the twenty-seven senators who voted against the Civil Rights Act were Southern segregationists. Goldwater was not a segregationist, nor was he any kind of racist. He was, in fact, a lifelong opponent of racial discrimination. At the beginning of his political career, as a city councilman, he had led the fight to end segregation in the Phoenix public schools; his first staff assistant when he went to the Senate, as Perlstein tells us, was a black woman; he was a member of the N.A.A.C.P. Goldwater voted against the Civil Rights Act because he believed, as a conservative, that the federal government did not have the power to compel states to conform to its idea of racial equality, or to dictate to individuals whom they must associate with.

The decision tormented him. Before the vote, he asked for advice from a political ally and speechwriter for his campaign, William Rehnquist, then a Phoenix attorney. Rehnquist assured him that the bill was unconstitutional. Goldwater sought a second opinion from another member of his brain trust, Robert Bork, then a law professor at Yale. Bork wrote a seventy-five-page concurrence. It was with a heavy heart (to borrow a phrase of L.B.J.'s) that Goldwater cast his vote on civil rights. He was, Perlstein says, "a shaken man afraid he was signing his political death warrant, convinced that the Constitution offered him no other honorable choice."

Two weeks after the Civil Rights Act became law, there was a riot in Harlem, followed by riots in black neighborhoods in three New Jersey cities, Philadelphia, Rochester, Cleveland, and a suburb of Chicago—the "long hot summer" of 1964. Those were not Southern cities. Johnson figured he had written off the Southern white vote by championing the Civil Rights Act, but the last thing he wanted was to allow issues like open housing and school desegregation to dominate his campaign in the North—which is why he chose to make war and peace the theme of the election.

Johnson was correct: he had thrown away the South. The only states Goldwater won (apart from his home state of Arizona, which he carried by less than a percentage point) were in the Deep South. He did not win them by a little. Goldwater took eighty-seven per cent of the vote in Mississippi. His campaign realigned the South: four of the five Southern states that he carried—Mississippi, Alabama, South Carolina, and Georgia—had gone to Adlai Stevenson eight years earlier. Eisenhower took forty per cent of the black vote in 1956; Nixon took thirty-two per cent in 1960. Goldwater got six per cent, and no Republican Presidential candidate since has equalled Nixon's percentage. George W. Bush, who made his convention a kind of diversity showcase, got eight per cent. Goldwater's longest-lasting political legacy was to drive African-Americans out of the party of Lincoln.

Goldwater did not want to campaign for the segregationist vote; he had even hoped that his personal opposition to discrimination would win him the votes of black people. But he had believed all along that the Southern white vote was basically conservative and potentially Republican. Republicans, he told Georgia activists in 1961, ought to stop chasing the votes of African-Americans and "go hunting where the ducks are." And the ducks in 1964 turned out to be white Democrats in revolt against integration. Goldwater's campaign slogan, "In Your Heart You Know He's Right," was an arrow aimed directly at them. It was a clear allusion to a prejudice that dared not speak its name.

All columnists should probably make it a rule to take off the week after a Presidential election. The temptation to prophecy is too distracting. An election, after all, is just a cup snatched from the stream of public opinion; while editorialists and commentators are peering into its depths, the river is rushing ceaselessly on. After the 1964 election, it did not seem likely that an alliance of single-issue voters—Southern segregationists and right-wing anti-Communists—could sustain a national party, because no one could have foreseen the combination of black militancy on civil rights and military futility in Vietnam that would split the Democratic Party. Liberals in 1964 thought that the Civil Rights Act would end racial strife, not (as it came to seem) inflame it; and Johnson had promised repeatedly, during the campaign, that he sought "no wider war" in Vietnam. Political predictions are generally based on the premise "If present trends continue," and it is in the nature of trends to change.

People did understand the 1964 election to mark a historic realignment; they just got the pieces backward. They believed that Goldwater's loss had aligned the Republican Party right out of the mainstream. In a Letter from Washington, in *The New Yorker*, datelined November 8, 1964, Richard Rovere asked if the Party could ever reconstitute itself. There are a few Republicans, he reported, "who think it can never be done, and not very many who think they will have more than half a party by 1968." Rovere tended to agree—he called his book on the election "The Goldwater Caper"—and most establishment columnists drew the same conclusion. Goldwater "has wrecked his party for a long time to come," James Reston informed readers of the *Times*.

It did not take a generation for this wisdom to be debunked. Goldwater's extremism and his success in the South disgusted Republican leaders in 1964—except one. Richard Nixon did not spurn the conservative grass-roots organization that Goldwater had put together to seize the nomination, and he learned from him the value of appealing to white anxiety about integration. Nixon did not carry the Deep South in 1968; George Wallace did. In a number of Southern states, Nixon ran behind Hubert Humphrey. But he put together a winning coalition for the Republican Party by attracting voters whose views the establishment considered reactionary and divisive. A year after Nixon's election, Kevin Phillips published his classic study "The Emerging Republican Majority," and there was suddenly a new paradigm for understanding the American electorate. You can see the difference Goldwater made to his party just by reading the list of politicians who once seemed to represent its ideological core and brightest political hopes, establishment darlings all: William Scranton, George Romney, Henry Cabot Lodge, Charles Percy, John Lindsay, Nelson Rockefeller. They have few heirs.

The politician who truly stands on the shoulders of Barry Goldwater is, of course, Ronald Reagan. During the campaign, Reagan sometimes opened or closed (occasionally opened *and* closed) on the bill with Goldwater. Audiences who found Goldwater edgy and aggressive found Reagan inspirational. And, on the Tuesday before the election, Reagan made his national political début with a spellbinding thirty-minute television commercial for Goldwater. It may not have won Goldwater many votes, but it put Reagan on the map. Two years later, he was elected governor of California, a state Johnson had carried by nearly 1.3 million votes.

Goldwater was uncharacteristically jealous. He never thanked Reagan for making the spot. But Reagan's effectiveness, as Perlstein shows, was the result of a strategy that would never have occurred to Goldwater or to his inflexibly doctrinaire campaign advisers. Reagan attacked the big-government policies of Franklin Roosevelt in what was in every other way a complete imitation of a Roosevelt fireside chat. He even used the New Deal phrase "rendezvous with destiny" to apostrophize the conservative crusade to end the era of New Deal-style government. He made small government sound just as warm and caring as big government. Reagan gave conservatism a human face, and this is why it makes more sense to call Goldwater a premonition rather than a cause of political change in America. A long stretch separates the Goldwater debacle of 1964 from the Reagan landslide of 1980, and one of the things that made that landslide possible was an accident of personality. Whatever their philosophical affinities, Reagan and Goldwater were, in one crucial area, as unalike as possible: Reagan was an actor.

Still, the tragedy of Barry Goldwater is also, in a sense, the tragedy of American conservatism, even in its moments of triumph. Goldwater's mistake was to believe that he could run for President on a program of political abstractions. People don't vote for abstractions. They vote their hopes and their fears, and they tend to see those in concrete terms. Twentieth-century American conservatism of the kind that Goldwater represented is the view that individuals, businesses, and local communities should have more control over their own destinies, and should not be subject to the bureaucratic dictates of a central government. As a proposition in political philosophy, there is no doubt much to be said for this view. Its appeal as a piece of campaign rhetoric obviously has everything to do with which bureaucratic dictates voters think you are talking about. What Goldwater discovered was that, in 1964, the philosophical language of conservatism was read by many voters as code for resistance to forced integration, and in the end he could not disentangle himself from an alliance that he had not desired. Many conservatives have found themselves in the same box. A political philosophy is not going to get very far without voters. As Goldwater observed, in politics you have to hunt where the ducks are. Most ducks are not interested in philosophy.

How accurate is the claim that the Goldwater campaign signalled the beginning of the end of the belief that government can solve social problems? The last Presidential election was essentially a contest between an advocate of smaller government and an advocate of active government. The advocate of smaller government is now President, it's true, but the advocate of active government got more votes. It still seems fair to say that it's not the principles most voters care about; it's the results. When, in the nineteen-fifties and sixties, big government seemed to be benefitting white Americans, they were generally liberal. Then, having got what they wanted, many of these people were willing to stuff the genie of big government back into the bottle. It seems unlikely, though, that they will throw away the bottle. ♦
