# The MAGA New Right: A Historical Arc

*This page is the master synthesis of the wiki. Read it first. Follow the wikilinks to go deeper.*

---

## The Central Question

How did American conservatism become the MAGA New Right? The surface answer — Trump happened — explains nothing. The real answer requires tracing a seventy-year arc in which a movement that called itself conservative gradually became something it had always secretly been: counterrevolutionary. The arc runs from Russell Kirk's Burkean prescriptivism in the 1950s through the Bradford-Jaffa constitutional civil war of the 1970s-80s, through the paleoconservative turn of the 1990s, to the full MAGA synthesis of the 2010s.

Understanding this arc requires three analytical tools: (1) the distinction between genuine conservatism and [[Counterrevolution|counterrevolution]]; (2) the constitutional genealogy of [[Declarationism]] and why it beat out neo-Confederatism; and (3) the mechanics of catastrophism — the rhetorical engine that licenses each escalation.

---

## I. The Conservative Baseline

Genuine conservatism, as theorized by [[Russell Kirk]] (*The Conservative Mind*, 1953) and the British philosopher Michael Oakeshott, is a *disposition*, not a program. It attaches to what exists. It prefers the familiar to the unknown, the tried to the untried. It sets low horizons for political action and is deeply suspicious of deliberate social engineering — including engineering aimed at *restoration*. Oakeshott: the conservative "is unadventurous."

Crucially, Kirk assumed the American constitutional order was basically intact. The republic had not been fundamentally transformed; there was something to conserve. His title *The Conservative Mind* was almost *The Conservative Rout* — he felt modernity had pushed conservatives back — but he believed the tides were turning.

This Burkean baseline matters because the Conservative Movement almost immediately departed from it.

---

## II. Catastrophism and the Counterrevolutionary Turn

The *National Review* prospectus (1955) characterized the New Deal as a "total revolution." That single move — declaring the republic already lost — made genuine Burkean conservation logically impossible. If the regime has fundamentally changed, what is there to conserve? The only available posture is restoration: getting back what was taken.

This is [[Counterrevolution]], and the American Conservative Movement has been functionally counterrevolutionary from the beginning, even while sailing under the conservative flag. The mechanism that drives the conversion is **catastrophism** — the insistence that the hour is late, the situation is dire, normal political norms no longer apply.

Catastrophism appears at every escalation point in the arc:
- *National Review* (1955): the New Deal was a "total revolution"
- Pat Buchanan (1992): "there is a religious war going on in our country"
- [[The Flight 93 Election]] (2016): vote Trump or the plane crashes
- [[Thomas Klingenstein]] (2022): "if you're actually in a war, even if it's a cold war, you behave differently... In war, you don't negotiate until you've won"

Each catastrophist claim licenses the next escalation. The logical endpoint — visible in Glenn Ellmers's Claremont essay declaring the 80 million Biden voters "not Americans in any meaningful sense of the term" — is a [[Decisionism|Schmittian]] friend/enemy distinction applied to half the electorate.

The most stubborn objection to this continuity reading has been the canonical "Buckley purged the fringe" story — the claim that [[William F. Buckley Jr.]]'s 1961–1965 *National Review* editorials excommunicated the [[John Birch Society]] and disciplined the conspiracist, racially reactionary right out of mainstream conservatism, drawing a real boundary that only collapsed with Trump. Recent historiography ([[Debunking a Longstanding Myth About William F. Buckley|Dallek 2023]], drawing on Schlozman and Rosenfeld) treats this as substantially mythologized: Buckley's "excommunication" was a *cordon sanitaire* drawn around [[Robert Welch]] personally while Buckley wrote privately to Birch leaders that he had not made "a single unfavorable reference to any members of the John Birch Society." The subscriber overlap, the personal relationships with antisemitic figures like Revilo Oliver, and the substantive Bircher politics — anti–civil rights, anti-UN nationalism, conspiracism, racial demographic anxiety — all stayed inside the conservative tent. The MAGA New Right is then legible not as the return of what was once expelled but as the resurfacing of what was always there once the brand-management consensus collapsed.

## III. The Constitutional Civil War: Bradford vs. Jaffa

The move from implicit to explicit counterrevolution required a constitutional theory — a story about what the republic *was* before it was corrupted, and therefore what must be restored. Inside the postwar right, two incompatible frameworks competed for this role.

**[[M.E. Bradford]]** represented the neo-Confederate tradition: honest about its premises, politically doomed. Bradford, a University of Dallas literature professor and Reagan's original nominee to head the NEH, attacked Abraham Lincoln as a Jacobin centralizer — a man who "misread" the Declaration's equality clause as a "deferred promise" and unleashed an endless series of egalitarian crusades. Lincoln's Declaration-based politics was "the Old Liberalism hidden under a Union battle flag." The Civil Rights revolution was its logical and catastrophic terminus. Bradford's constitutional alternative: the South was right. Equality (capital E) is "the antonym of every legitimate conservative principle."

**[[Harry V. Jaffa]]** represented the [[Declarationism|Declarationist]] alternative: Lincoln as philosopher-statesman, the Declaration as the Constitution's beating heart. Jaffa's Lincoln is the republic's redeemer — the man who revealed what "all men are created equal" had always meant and completed the Founding by ending slavery. The Declaration's natural rights principles are the constitutional lodestar against which all progressive departures must be measured.

The Bradford-Jaffa debate was one of the foundational intellectual conflicts of postwar American conservatism. Both men were looking for a constitutional theory that could resist the administrative state and progressive constitutional revision. They could not both be right.

## IV. Why Jaffa Won — and What It Cost

The key insight from Ken Kersch's [[Beyond Originalism]] (2011): Jaffa won not because he was more philosophically correct, but because [[Declarationism]] was *politically viable* in a way neo-Confederatism was not after 1965.

Bradford's constitutional politics were too honestly Southern. When Reagan nominated Bradford to head the NEH in 1981, neoconservatives (Irving Kristol, Norman Podhoretz) blocked it by pointing to his Lincoln attacks and George Wallace support. William Bennett was appointed instead. That was the institutional turning point.

Declarationism then did the ideological work Bradford had wanted — resisting progressive constitutional expansion — while wrapping it in Lincoln and Martin Luther King Jr. rather than Calhoun and Lee. This is the **Declarationist Triptych**: Lincoln, Jefferson, and MLK invoked together as witnesses to America's eternal return to its founding principles. By appropriating the civil rights tradition, Declarationists could attack civil rights *policies* (affirmative action, disparate impact, DEI) while claiming the mantle of civil rights *principles*.

Kersch identifies two simultaneous functions: (1) it morally rehabilitates Southern conservatism, allowing the right to claim the post-1965 high ground without abandoning its anti-egalitarian commitments; (2) it unifies the Religious Right by giving Catholics and evangelicals a shared constitutional language — natural rights rather than sectarian theology. Both functions are crucial to the modern Republican Party coalition.

**The cost:** Declarationism required accepting Lincoln and, at least nominally, his universalist equality principle. The [[Claremont Institute]], founded by Jaffa's students, became the intellectual vanguard of Trumpism. But Trumpism's actual political commitments — ethnic nationalism, immigration restriction, nostalgia for pre-Civil Rights constitutional arrangements — sit in obvious tension with the Declarationist framework. Philip Jaffa (Harry's son) charges the institute with betrayal of his father's teachings, pointing to Christopher Caldwell's sympathetic *CRB* essay on Robert E. Lee as evidence that Bradford's ghost is returning inside the Declarationist house.

## V. The Paleocon Transition

[[Paleoconservatism]] in the 1980s-90s is where the Declarationist victory met its cultural limits. Pat Buchanan and Sam Francis recognized what Reagan's presidency had proved: electoral victories had not stopped the sexual revolution, the demographic transformation of America, or the advance of feminism. The Constitution could be reclaimed in the courts; the culture could not be reclaimed by winning elections.

Sam Francis's essay on the Conservative Movement as **"Beautiful Losers"** named the problem: the movement had won political battles (Reagan, tax cuts, the Cold War) while consistently losing the cultural war it cared about most. His diagnosis: the movement had been too genteel, too willing to play by liberal rules. The counterrevolutionary impulse — always present but kept implicit — needed to become explicit.

Buchanan's 1992 "culture war" speech was the announcement. The target was now specific cultural changes — abortion, homosexuality, feminism, demographic displacement — that the movement argued had to be reversed. Bradford's instincts were vindicated culturally even as his constitutional framework had been rejected institutionally. The explicit MAGA rhetoric about demographic "invasion," about restoring a prior cultural order — this is Bradford's politics without Bradford's honesty about its neo-Confederate roots.

## VI. The MAGA Synthesis

The MAGA New Right is a coalition of counterrevolutionary currents that had been developing separately for decades. Trump's function — or vacancy — was to become the vehicle that held them together without forcing any single current to state its premises explicitly:

- **[[The Claremonters]]**: Declarationist constitutional theory; catastrophism about the administrative state; the intellectual vanguard that gave Trump legitimacy
- **[[Postliberalism]]**: [[Patrick Deneen]], [[Adrian Vermeule]], [[Sohrab Ahmari]]; explicit rejection of liberal proceduralism; use state power for substantive cultural goods
- **[[Christian Nationalism]]**: [[Mike Johnson]], Stephen Wolfe; CFE and Independent Charismatic networks; dominionist politics
- **[[National Conservatism]]**: [[Yoram Hazony]], [[Christopher DeMuth]]; nationalist foreign policy; [[Viktor Orbán]] as model
- **[[Hard Right]] adjacent**: [[Costin Alamariu]] (BAP), [[Curtis Yarvin]]; not formally integrated but providing the energy the movement channels into the mainstream via figures like [[Michael Anton]] and outlets like *The American Mind*

[[Steve Bannon]]'s self-description as a "Leninist" — wanting to "destroy the state" — was the honest version. [[Thomas Klingenstein]]'s "woke communism" and "cold civil war" framing was the institutional version. [[The Flight 93 Election]] was the popular version. They are all expressions of the same catastrophist counterrevolutionary logic.

The clearest single institutional indicator of how complete the synthesis has become is the trajectory of the [[Heritage Foundation]]. In 2007, Heritage's flagship intellectual-history report ([[The Conservative Consensus]] by Lee Edwards) prescribed "**a renewed fusionism**" — limited government, free markets, individual freedom and responsibility, virtue cultivated through a balanced civic order. The same institution under [[Kevin Roberts]] (a Buchanan-1992 volunteer) published [[Project 2025]] in 2023 — a 920-page operational program for state-led cultural restoration via [[Schedule F]] and [[Administrative State]] dismantling. The 2007 prescription was structured precisely to forestall the 2023 program. Heritage has not just changed leadership; it has switched between two structurally incompatible frameworks. The same building, same donor base, same Mandate-for-Leadership format — but the political philosophy has flipped from Meyer-Reagan-Buckley fusionism to a Bozell-Deneen-Vermeule postliberalism. The wiki treats this single trajectory as one of the most useful indicators of the systemic shift the MAGA New Right represents at the institutional level.

## VII. The Unresolved Contradiction

The wiki's developing thesis: the MAGA New Right is incoherent in a specific, traceable way. It inherited Jaffa's constitutional *language* (Declarationism, Lincoln, natural rights) while pursuing Bradford's actual *political goals* (demographic nationalism, cultural restoration, hostility to egalitarianism).

This contradiction is visible at the institutional level. The [[Claremont Institute]] nominally maintains Jaffa's Declarationist framework while publishing Glenn Ellmers's Schmittian friend/enemy declarations and Christopher Caldwell's Lee hagiography. Philip Jaffa charges betrayal. [[Thomas Klingenstein]]'s war rhetoric is Bradford's politics in Jaffa's clothes.

[[Adrian Vermeule]]'s [[Common Good Constitutionalism]] makes the contradiction explicit from the other direction: he rejects Declarationism (and its natural rights liberalism) as the *source* of the administrative state it purports to oppose. If Vermeule is right, Jaffa's project was always self-defeating — the natural rights language that seemed to provide a constitutional alternative to progressive egalitarianism actually enabled it.

The question the wiki is tracking: is the New Right producing a coherent political philosophy capable of governing, or is it a coalition of catastrophisms that will dissolve once the shared enemy (liberal administrative progressivism) is defeated or entrenched enough to no longer serve as unifying glue?

[[Laura K. Field]]'s *[[Furious Minds]]* — the anchor text — is pessimistic: the intellectual leaders of the New Right are more interested in winning than in governing, and their appeals to the founding function as legitimizing rhetoric for a power project that the founding would not recognize.
