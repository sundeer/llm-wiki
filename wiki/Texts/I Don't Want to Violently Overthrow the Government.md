# "'I Don't Want to Violently Overthrow the Government. I Want Something Far More Revolutionary.'"

**Ian Ward (*Politico Magazine*, 8 June 2023). Genre: long-form profile of [[Patrick Deneen]] occasioned by the publication of *Regime Change* (2023). The wiki's principal source for Deneen's biography, intellectual formation, and the postliberal vocabulary he has been crystallizing since *Why Liberalism Failed* (2018) — including the terms "**common-good conservatism**," "**aristopopulism**," and "**Party of Order vs. Party of Progress**" — and for the documentation of his entanglement with [[JD Vance]], [[Kevin Roberts]], and the [[Postliberal Order]] cohort.**

---

## What the Profile Adds

The article's opening scene (250 conservative-intelligentsia attendees at Catholic University; Vance entering and immediately hugging Deneen; a panel that included [[Kevin Roberts]] and *Modern Age* editor Daniel McCarthy) is the most vivid available documentation of postliberalism's institutional arrival in Washington. By 2023 Deneen is no longer a contrarian academic; he is the philosophical figurehead the New Right's elected officials physically embrace.

### Biographical Sources of Deneen's Postliberalism

Ward traces the through-line:

- **Catholic upbringing** in Windsor, Connecticut.
- **Rutgers undergraduate (early 1980s).** Met [[Wilson Carey McWilliams]], the communitarian political theorist whose 1973 *The Idea of Fraternity in America* taught Deneen there was "an important and undervalued counter-tradition in American politics that speaks the language of fraternity and friendship, of community and citizenship." McWilliams's politics were "unapologetically left."
- **Christopher Lasch** (read in graduate school): an iconoclastic social critic whose anti-liberal, populist-religious-tradition framework Deneen absorbed.
- **Princeton assistant professor (1997–2004).** Surrounded by Rawlsian liberals; *denied tenure in 2004* — Deneen suspects partly for his anti-liberalism. "I was shown the door." Romance (Deneen's friend): Princeton "may have reinforced and intensified his already strong dislike of liberalism."
- **Wilson Carey McWilliams's death** (March 2005): "When Carey died, he lost something that centered him."
- **Georgetown (2005–2012).** Founded the Tocqueville Forum; opened with an inaugural lecture by Antonin Scalia (2006). Started writing about peak oil theory (2007); the 2008 financial crisis vindicated his "natural limits" critique without requiring the specific peak-oil claim. Co-founded *Front Porch Republic* (2008).
- **Notre Dame (2012–present).** *Why Liberalism Failed* (January 2018). Met [[Viktor Orbán]] in Budapest, autumn 2019.
- **The Postliberal Order Substack** (Nov 2021) co-launched with [[Adrian Vermeule]], [[Gladden Pappin]], Chad Pecknold. *Compact* (Mar 2022), launched by [[Sohrab Ahmari]].

The McWilliams biography is the wiki's most important new datum on Deneen's intellectual formation: his *original* commitments are to a left-leaning communitarianism, a critique of corporate-managerial elites, and a defense of localism. The shift to a state-power postliberalism is therefore not the natural extension of those commitments but a significant departure that his Rutgers cohort regards with concern. McWilliams's daughter (who studied under Deneen at Princeton) explicitly worries that Deneen has "lost sight of an essential component of [her] father's thinking" — that there are alternatives to liberalism "much more frightening" than its defects.

### New Vocabulary Deneen Crystallizes in *Regime Change*

- **Common-good conservatism**: Deneen's preferred name for his framework. Distinct from Vermeule's [[Common Good Constitutionalism]] (which it overlaps with) and from [[National Conservatism]] (which it explicitly rejects). Deneen: "It's not that any of us is anti-nation, but there has to be something both less than and more than the nation" — local communities + "specifically Catholic notion of universal humanity."
- **Aristopopulism**: an alliance between *postliberal elites* and *conservative populists* — non-elites with substantively traditional commitments, led by elites who share their values. The pseudo-Machiavellian framing: a "peaceful but vigorous overthrow of a corrupt and corrupting liberal ruling class," resulting in a regime "superficially the same" as the current order but informed by "a fundamentally different ethos."
- **Party of Order vs. Party of Progress**: Deneen's binary recasting of American political conflict. "Party of Progress" = liberal and conservative *elites* aligned around social and economic "progress." "Party of Order" = non-elites who want unions, anti-corporate checks, abortion limits, religion in the public sphere, and an end to "wokeness." The terms are an unmarked borrowing from 19th-century French Saint-Simonian and Comtean vocabulary; the Saint-Simonian "Party of Order" was the proto-authoritarian counter-party to revolutionary republicanism.

### Policy Proposals from *Regime Change*

Deneen lists transitional policies that could "dilute the power of the current ruling class" before regime change comes to pass:

- Expand the size of the U.S. House of Representatives.
- "Break up" Washington by redistributing federal agencies around the country.
- Strengthen labor unions; expand industrial policy.
- Create a "Family Czar" to promote family formation.
- Tax elite-college endowments.
- Restrict or outright abolish the sale of pornography.

Field's verdict (quoted by Ward): "I have not seen a single policy put forward by [the postliberals] that wouldn't be better pursued within the existing liberal democratic framework, so the idea of an overhaul of the actual regime seems really unnecessarily provocative and reckless."

### Field's Frame: "Illiberal Constitutionalism"

[[Laura K. Field]] tells Ward that "fascist" is the wrong term and offers instead "**illiberal constitutionalism**" — "a sort of halfway house between liberal democracy and traditional authoritarianism that maintains the trappings of a liberal regime while dramatically expanding the power of the state." This is the same frame she develops at book length in [[Furious Minds]]. The Ward profile is the most concise public statement of it.

### The Title

Deneen, addressing his Catholic audience: "I don't want to violently overthrow the government. I want something far more revolutionary than that." The line is the cleanest single statement of the postliberal pitch — the project is *more* radical than January 6, not *less*; what's new is the refusal of violent means in favor of structural capture of state and cultural institutions.

When Ward asks directly whether January 6 would be an example of acceptable Machiavellian tactics, Deneen says: "For me, it wouldn't be." The wiki's structural reading: that "for me" is doing significant work. It is a personal disavowal, not a categorical one.
