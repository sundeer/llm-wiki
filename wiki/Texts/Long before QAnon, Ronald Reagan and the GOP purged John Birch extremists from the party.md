# Long before QAnon, Ronald Reagan and the GOP purged John Birch extremists from the party

**Erick Trickey (*Washington Post* "Retropolis," 15 January 2021). Genre: popular history article, published nine days after January 6. Retells the standard "Buckley purge" narrative as a guide for what mainstream Republicans should now be doing to QAnon. The wiki preserves it as the canonical statement of the myth that [[Debunking a Longstanding Myth About William F. Buckley]] (Dallek, 2023) demolishes.**

---

## Core Argument

Trickey's framing is explicit and contemporary: the post–January 6 Republican Party faces a choice "much like" the one Buckley, Goldwater, and Reagan faced with the [[John Birch Society]] in the early 1960s. The piece presents the 1961–1965 sequence as a successful precedent: the leaders disciplined the conspiracist fringe, the JBS went into "steep decline," and the conservative movement was protected for decades. The implicit argument: the GOP could and should do the same with QAnon if it has the will.

---

## Key Claims (the standard "purge" narrative)

- **The 1962 Palm Beach meeting.** Goldwater met [[William F. Buckley Jr.]] and [[Russell Kirk]] in a hotel suite at the Breakers in Palm Beach. Kirk (per Buckley's 2008 *Commentary* recollection) said the JBS "should be renounced by Goldwater and by everyone else… with any influence on the conservative movement." Buckley told Goldwater the society was a "menace" to the movement.
- **Goldwater's pushback.** "Every other person in Phoenix is a member of the John Birch Society… I'm not talking about commie-haunted apple pickers or cactus drunks. I'm talking about the highest cast of men of affairs."
- **The compromise.** Buckley and Kirk agreed to attack [[Robert Welch]] personally without criticizing rank-and-file members, giving Goldwater political cover.
- **Buckley's first editorial.** A 5,000-word *National Review* editorial criticized Welch's views as "so far removed from common sense." Goldwater followed with a published letter calling Welch's views "far removed from reality and common sense" and denying that they represented the JBS membership.
- **Initial failure.** "Extremism in the defense of liberty is no vice" — Goldwater's 1964 acceptance speech, plus the convention's rejection of an anti-extremist plank, contributed to his landslide loss to Lyndon Johnson.
- **The 1965 escalation.** Buckley denounced JBS's *American Opinion* in his syndicated column for calling Selma civil rights protesters "a horde of termites" and accusing State, Justice, and the Supreme Court of communist control. Hate mail and subscription cancellations followed; Buckley "doubled down."
- **Reagan's "I am not a member."** September 1965, at a Republican fundraiser before his 1966 California gubernatorial run: "I am not a member. I have no intention of becoming a member. I am not going to solicit their support." A "lunatic fringe," he said, had infiltrated the JBS.
- **The institutional break.** A week later, Republican congressional leaders Everett Dirksen and Gerald Ford jointly denounced the JBS. Buckley's *National Review* ran a 14-page anti-Bircher special section in October 1965. Goldwater wrote a new letter calling on conservatives to leave the JBS if Welch did not resign.
- **The claimed result.** Quoting Carl T. Bogus's 2013 biography of Buckley: "No fig leaf of respectability remained. The society went into steep decline. Recruiting new members was exceedingly difficult. … Even highly visible members quit." Trickey concludes: "The conservative movement had excommunicated the Birchers' conspiratorial, unpatriotic hostility — for the next few decades, at least."

---

## Why the Wiki Treats This as the Foil

The Trickey piece is internally honest — it credits Goldwater's reluctance, notes the Reagan reversal as buzz-driven rather than principled, and qualifies the result with "for the next few decades, at least." But its overall frame — gatekeepers held the line, the line eventually broke — is the standard mythologized history that [[Matthew Dallek]] takes apart in *Birchers* (2023).

The piece is preserved here as **the clearest statement of the narrative the wiki rejects**, useful as a foil. It also documents key facts (the Palm Beach meeting, the chronology of editorials, Reagan's exact wording) that survive Dallek's revisionism — Dallek does not deny the editorials happened, he denies that they accomplished what the standard story claims.

---

## Where Trickey and Dallek Agree

- The Palm Beach 1962 meeting happened (Buckley, Kirk, Goldwater).
- Buckley targeted Welch personally rather than the JBS as such.
- Goldwater sought to preserve Bircher rank-and-file support throughout 1964.
- Reagan's September 1965 disavowal was politically motivated (the gubernatorial race and growing buzz, not principled rejection).
- Buckley's October 1965 *NR* special intensified the attack and lost subscribers.

## Where They Diverge

- **What the campaign accomplished.** Trickey: a real "excommunication" that pushed the JBS into "steep decline" and protected the movement for decades. Dallek: a public-relations cordon around Welch personally, while the rank-and-file, the magazine subscriber overlap, and the substantive Bircher politics (anti-civil rights, conspiracism, anti-UN nationalism) remained inside the tent.
- **What it means for now.** Trickey: precedent for an effective post-January 6 GOP purge. Dallek: warning that even the canonical purge wasn't really a purge.
