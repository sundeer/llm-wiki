# Iberian Vistas: Franco, Salazar, and American Conservatives

**José Pedro Zúquete (Portuguese political scientist) and George Hawley (American political scientist; *Right-Wing Critics of American Conservatism*, 2016; *The Moderate Majority*, 2024), *Politics, Religion & Ideology* (Sage), 2025; the academic synthesis tracing the line from postwar [[National Review|*NR*]]-circle Catholic admiration of [[Francisco Franco]] and [[António Salazar]] to the contemporary [[Postliberalism|postliberal]] / [[Catholic Integralism|integralist]] revival of those same regimes as institutional models.**

---

## Why It Matters Here

Iberian Vistas is the wiki's primary source for a *previously untraced* through-line in the synthesis: the postwar conservative movement's Iberian-authoritarian Catholic admiration as the direct ancestor of contemporary [[Adrian Vermeule|Vermeule]] / [[Gladden Pappin|Pappin]] / [[Patrick Deneen|Deneen]] anti-liberal political-form thinking. Pre–Iberian Vistas, the wiki traced postliberalism through Bozell-1962 and Catholic intellectual currents but did not have the *concrete authoritarian regime models* the postwar Catholics were holding up. With Iberian Vistas, we can name them: **Franco's Spain and Salazar's *Estado Novo* are the regime types postwar Catholic conservatives admired and modern postliberals are reviving.**

The paper also adds a substantial node-set to the wiki's thinker map: [[Willmoore Kendall]], [[James Burnham]], [[Erik von Kuehnelt-Leddihn]], [[Thomas Molnar]] (the 1950s–60s admirers); [[Charles Haywood]], [[Josh Abbotoy]], [[Edmund Waldstein]], [[Christopher Roach]], [[Helen Andrews]], [[Joseph Pearce]], [[Michael Knowles]] (the 2010s–20s revivalists).

---

## Core Argument

Two periods of American-conservative interest in Iberian authoritarianism:

### Period 1 — 1950s–60s (Cold War + Decolonization)

- **Demographic note.** Roman Catholics were "*massively* overrepresented" among postwar conservative intellectuals. Buckley, Kendall (Yale convert; Buckley's mentor), [[L. Brent Bozell Jr.|Bozell]] (convert), [[Russell Kirk]] (convert), [[Frank Meyer]] (convert at end of life), [[James Burnham]] (convert at end of life). This is the wiki's strongest prior under-emphasis: the postwar movement's *Catholic* center of gravity.
- **Personal Iberian connections.** Buckley's father had Mexican oil interests; *Spanish was Buckley's first language*. Kendall and Bozell lived in Franco's Spain.
- **Franco as Christian-anti-communist hero.** Buckley (1957): "General Franco is an authentic national hero… [who] wrest[ed] Spain out of the hands of visionaries, ideologues, Marxists and nihilists." James Burnham (1975 obituary): "the most successful ruler" of the twentieth century. Allitt: many American Catholics viewed Franco as "the George Washington of Spain."
- **Salazar.** Dean Acheson (1962, Democratic Sec of State) called him "the nearest approach in our time to Plato's philosopher-king." 1956 *NR* published Salazar's "Be resolved to fight." Buckley (1958): "we do not despise Salazar for using force to keep his highly benevolent regime in power."
- **Decolonization.** Burnham, Buckley, Kuehnelt-Leddihn, and Molnar published serial *NR* defenses of Portuguese colonialism in Angola and Mozambique. Kuehnelt-Leddihn (a year before the Colonial War began): "the only part of Africa devoid of racial tensions lies in the area controlled by a power which does *not* believe in formal democracy."
- **Jeffrey Hart's Guernica denial (1973 *NR*)** — argued the Guernica bombing never happened. Editors gave space to historians to push back but framed it as a real debate.
- **The Bozell exception.** Bozell wanted to *import* Franco's model. *Triumph* magazine (1966–1976) was the venue. He was the only American conservative who openly advocated Catholic theocracy. He lost the argument; *Triumph* folded.

### Decline (1980s–2000s)

Iberian admiration faded as Spain liberalized post-Franco, the Cold War ended, and neoconservatism displaced paleoconservatism inside the movement. [[Allan Bloom]] (1987): Throne-and-Altar conservatism "breathed its last only with Francisco Franco in 1975."

### Period 2 — 2010s–present (postliberal revival)

- **Charles Haywood** (independent Hard-Right blogger, post-business-fortune): "my impression of [Franco] is positively glowing." On Salazar: "nothing notable or worthwhile has happened in Portugal since Salazar died" — and key for the wiki: "for post-liberals in particular, Salazar is necessarily interesting, since he is one of the few twentieth-century examples of a long-lived Right regime that successfully opposed the corrosion of Enlightenment liberalism."
- **Josh Abbotoy**, "Is a Protestant Franco inevitable?" (*First Things*, 2023): "If the current trajectory holds, it is certainly possible that conditions will deteriorate until something like a Protestant Franco becomes inevitable."
- **Christopher Roach**, "The Salazar Option" (*American Greatness*, 2021): "use of State power at the service of a counterrevolution"; take off the "kid gloves"; reject the Benedict Option.
- **Helen Andrews** (*First Things*, 2021, on Tom Gallagher's Salazar biography): "The difference is that our corporative actors answer to no higher authority. The Portuguese had Salazar single-handedly mediating among society's various interests… Without these external constraints, rule by elites is not corporatism but oligarchy."
- **Michael Warren Davis** (*The American Conservative*, 2021): "if liberalism *is* off the table, the only alternative to a tyrant like Lenin or Hitler may be a man like Salazar: a paternalistic traditionalist, a philosopher king."
- **Adrian Vermeule.** Cites Salazar's Portugal as a country where "bureaucracy has flourished in an illiberal regime"; advocates focus on "executive-type bureaucracies rather than on parliamentary-democratic institutions *per se*." Direct connection: Salazar's *Estado Novo* is Vermeule's working analogue for a Schedule-F-and-administrative-state-captured United States.
- **Gladden Pappin.** "Corporatism for the Twenty-First Century" (2020): defends a corporate alternative in political representation and education; advocates "a state-led program to establish the corporate bodies of society." The corporatist organizational form Pappin advocates is *Estado Novo* corporatism, ported.
- **Counter-voices.** Peter Hitchens (*First Things* 2019), Richard Reinsch (*Law and Liberty* 2023), Cameron Hilditch (*NR* 2021): the Franco/Salazar admiration is "self-radicalized derangement" and "long and dark valley." David Frum and integralist purists (Pater Edmund Waldstein on tactical grounds; Domencic) also pushed back.
- **Michael Knowles** (*Daily Wire*): "Franco was pretty good." [[Joseph Pearce]] (*Imaginative Conservative*, 2023): "The Christians of Spain had saved Europe from the infidel in the Civil War as they had saved Europe from the Islamic infidel eight hundred years earlier."

## Major Lessons (Zúquete & Hawley's Synthesis)

1. **Executive-power primacy.** Both regimes give the wiki a name for the political form Vermeule and Pappin advocate — concentrated executive authority pursuing substantive moral ends, with parliamentary democracy demoted.
2. **Corporatism as the institutional alternative.** The *Estado Novo*'s corporatist representation (interests organized by sector, mediated by the state) is the model behind Pappin's contemporary advocacy.
3. **Why the revival is structurally limited.** The US is far less religious than the Catholic Iberia of 1950–70. A Catholic-theocratic transformation is "less believable than ever" (Hawley, 2017; Jones, 2016 demographics). Bozell's effort failed in 1976; the post-Bozell version is "even more quixotic."
4. **But the *intellectual* significance is real.** "Debates about the nature of US conservatism formerly settled have been re-opened, and there is new ideological space for intellectual descendants of paleoconservatism and other more radical right-wing movements to grow." The very fact that Salazar and Franco are debated in *First Things* and *American Greatness* is the indicator.
5. **The framing connects to Field's [[Furious Minds|*Furious Minds*]].** Zúquete & Hawley cite Field (2024) on the integralist project as "radical" and bent on "counterrevolution." The two analyses are convergent.

---

## Methodological Note for the Wiki

Zúquete & Hawley are *not* writing a polemic — Hawley in particular is known for empirical-electoral work (*The Moderate Majority*, 2024, argues the Republican base is *less* radicalized than commentary suggests). Their conclusion that the Iberian-authoritarian revival is "potentially significant" but "limited" is an important brake on the wiki's catastrophist impulse: the new Hard-Right corporatism is real *as an intellectual project* but the constituency for actual Catholic theocracy is shrinking, not growing. The wiki should be careful not to inflate the Iberian thread beyond what the demographic data supports.
