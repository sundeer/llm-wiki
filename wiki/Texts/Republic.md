# Republic (*Politeia*)

**[[Plato]], c. 380 BCE; translated by G.M.A. Grube, revised by C.D.C. Reeve (Hackett, 1992); Plato's central political dialogue — a defense of justice as intrinsically good for the soul, told through the construction of an ideal city — and the deepest philosophical source for the New Right's anti-democratic political theory**

---

## Bibliographic Note

- **Author:** [[Plato]] (c. 428–348 BCE)
- **Date:** c. 380 BCE
- **Genre:** Philosophical dialogue; political philosophy
- **Translation:** G.M.A. Grube, revised by C.D.C. Reeve (Hackett Publishing, 1992)
- **Structure:** 10 books; approximately 293 pages of text in this edition
- **Interlocutors:** Socrates (narrator), Glaucon, Adeimantus, Thrasymachus, Polemarchus, Cephalus

---

## Central Argument

The Republic is structured as a response to Thrasymachus's claim that justice is "the advantage of the stronger" (Book I, 338c) and Glaucon's refinement: that the just person appearing unjust suffers more than the unjust person appearing just (Books I–II). To prove that justice is intrinsically good — good for the soul regardless of its rewards — Socrates proposes to read justice "writ large" in a city before finding it in the individual soul.

The dialogue constructs an ideal city (*kallipolis*) governed by philosopher-kings, shows that justice in the city means "each class doing its own work" in a natural hierarchy (432b), transfers this definition to the individual soul (which has three parts — rational, spirited, appetitive — mirroring the city's three classes), and then concludes that justice is the health of the soul regardless of consequences.

The dialogue ends with the Myth of Er (Book X) demonstrating that justice also leads to the best outcomes in the afterlife — responding to Glaucon's initial challenge by showing that justice is good both intrinsically and for its consequences.

## Book Summaries

### Book I: The Challenge
Thrasymachus argues justice is "the advantage of the stronger" — rulers make laws in their own interest and call obedience "justice" (338c). He adds that the completely unjust man (the tyrant) lives best: he has taken others' property, enslaved them, and everyone calls him happy (344a-c). Socrates refutes him through the craft analogy (rulers, like doctors, serve their subjects rather than themselves) and forces Thrasymachus to concede that justice is virtue. But Socrates acknowledges the argument is inconclusive.

### Book II: Glaucon's Challenge and the City's Foundation
Glaucon distinguishes three classes of goods: those good only for their consequences (medicine), those good only intrinsically (pleasure), and those good both intrinsically and for their consequences — where justice belongs, Socrates claims. Glaucon challenges this: **the Ring of Gyges thought experiment** (359d-360d) shows that if invisible, anyone would act unjustly. The fully unjust man appearing just is happier than the fully just man appearing unjust (360e-362b). Adeimantus adds: society praises justice only for its rewards and reputation, not for itself.

Socrates proposes to read justice "writ large" in a city. He constructs the kallipolis from necessity: a "healthy city" of simple craftsmen (372a-c) quickly becomes a "feverish city" requiring soldiers because of *pleonexia* — the insatiable desire for more (373a). War is natural to human acquisitiveness.

### Book III: Education, Censorship, and the Noble Lie
Guardians (soldiers-turned-rulers) must be educated through music/poetry that shows the gods as good and unchanging, humans as practicing moderation. False or debasing stories are censored. **The Noble Lie** (*Myth of Metals*, 414b-415d): citizens are to be told a "Phoenician tale" — that their upbringing was a dream, that they were formed in the earth, and that the god mixed gold in the souls of rulers, silver in auxiliaries, and iron/bronze in farmers and craftsmen. The metals don't always breed true: gold parents can have silver children and vice versa. The oracle warns the city will be ruined if iron/bronze becomes a guardian. Guardian housing: no private property, no gold or silver, communal meals — they hold gold "of a divine sort" in their souls (416c-417b).

### Book IV: The Four Virtues and the Tripartite Soul
Justice is defined as "each person doing their own work" — the city's three classes (rulers, soldiers, craftsmen) each in their proper role, with no meddling between them (432b-434c). Injustice = civil war between the parts; meddling = the greatest harm to the city. The same three-part structure is found in the individual soul: **rational** (logistikon), **spirited** (thymoeides), **appetitive** (epithymetikon). The appetitive part is the "largest" and "most insatiable for money" (439a). Justice in the individual = reason rules, spirit assists and obeys, appetite is governed.

Musical innovation as political danger: "Musical modes are never changed without change to the most important of a city's laws" (424c — citing Damon). Culture precedes politics.

### Books V–VII: The Three Waves and the Philosopher-King
Three "waves" of paradox: (1) women as guardians, (2) community of wives and children, (3) philosopher-kings. The first two introduce guardian communism (no private marriages or children; eugenics controlled by rulers through rigged lotteries, 459a-461d) and the body-politic metaphor (city as single organism, 462b). The third wave is the pivot:

**"Until philosophers rule as kings in cities... cities will have no rest from evils, nor, I think, will the human race."** (473c-d)

Philosophers are defined as those who love the sight of truth — they know the **Forms** themselves (what always is), while the many only *opine* about beautiful and ugly things that are also their opposites (479d). **Knowledge** (*episteme*) is set over what fully is; **opinion** (*doxa*) is set over what both is and is not. The many "roll around between being and not being" and can only opine.

**Ship of State** (488a-489d): The demos/shipowner is big and strong but hard of hearing and shortsighted; sailors quarrel for control; they drug/tie up the true navigator (philosopher) and call him "stargazer, babbler, useless." Democracy is rule by the incompetent choosing by flattery.

**The Great Beast** (492-493): The sophist learns the crowd's appetites and calls this wisdom. Philosophy is corrupted by the "many" who constitute the real sophists.

**The Sun analogy** (507a-509c): The Form of the Good is to the intelligible realm as the sun is to the visible — giving both truth to intelligibles and being itself to the Forms. "The good is not being but superior to it in rank and power" (509b).

**The Divided Line** (509d-511e): Four cognitive states — eikasia (images), pistis (belief in particulars), dianoia (mathematical thought using hypotheses), noesis (dialectical understanding of the unhypothetical first principle).

**The Cave Allegory** (514a-520a): Prisoners chained in a cave see only shadows on a wall; they name the shadows and think that is truth. A freed prisoner, dragged into sunlight, eventually sees the sun itself. Returning to the cave, his eyes dim; the prisoners mock him and refuse liberation — "if they could, they would kill him" (517a). The cave = visible world; fire = sun; sun = the Form of the Good; the ascent = philosophy.

**Education as periagoge** (turning, 518b-519b): Education is not putting sight into blind eyes but turning the whole soul toward the light. The virtue of reason is more divine — it doesn't become good or bad, only useful or harmful depending on its direction. The philosophers who have seen the Good must return to govern the cave, not because they want to, but because the law requires it — this makes the city better governed, since its rulers are not ambitious for power (519d-521b).

**The "quickest way"** (541a): If a philosopher-king gained power, he would send everyone over ten into the country and raise the children fresh — a tabula rasa political transformation.

### Book VIII: The Five Constitutions and Democratic Degeneration
The ideal city degenerates through four stages:

**Timocracy** (honor-loving, Spartan): Civil war breaks out when iron/bronze guardians mix with gold/silver; the resulting constitution honors the spirited part — war and victory over wisdom and music (547c-548d). The timocratic man is created by a good father who avoids politics and a mother who shames the son for his father's "unmanliness" (549c-d).

**Oligarchy** (wealth-based): Timocrats discover money; wealth becomes the measure of virtue. The city splits into rich and poor — "two cities at war" (422e, recalled here). A drone class of poor, indebted citizens accumulates, resentful and armed (553a-555b).

**Democracy** (freedom-based): The poor overcome the rich; the city is full of freedom and license. **"Like a coat embroidered with every kind of ornament, this city would seem the most beautiful"** (557c) — ironic: democracy is multicolored, tolerant, pleasant — and ultimately fatal. No requirement to rule or be ruled; condemned criminals walk free; everything is equal. The democratic man: the son of the thrifty oligarch who falls in with the drone class; all pleasures declared equal; **"he calls it pleasant, free, and blessedly happy"** (561d). "Insolence called good breeding; anarchy, freedom; extravagance, magnificence; shamelessness, courage" (560d-561a).

**The democratic degeneration to tyranny** (562a-569c): Democracy is destroyed by its own insatiable desire for freedom. Anyone who imposes any constraint is called an oligarch; freedom spreads until:
- Fathers behave like children, sons like fathers
- Teachers flatter students; old people imitate the young
- Slaves are nearly equal to their owners; animals roam freely
- Citizens' souls become so sensitive they cannot endure even the slightest degree of authority

**"Extreme freedom can't lead to anything but extreme slavery."** (564a) The disease of democratic permissiveness creates a drone class of demagogues who distribute wealth to the people while keeping most for themselves. The people set up one champion → he tastes blood (the Lycaean Zeus wolf-story, 565d) → becomes a full tyrant. In power, he smiles, makes promises, redistributes land, frees debt slaves — then stirs war so people feel need of a leader (566e-567b); purges the brave, knowledgeable, and rich; fills his bodyguard with "drones and slaves" (568a).

**"The people...have fallen from the frying pan of enslavement to free men into the fire of having slaves as their masters."** (569b-c)

### Book IX: The Tyrant's Psychology and the Proofs of Justice
The tyrannical soul is driven by **erotic love as a great winged drone** (573a) — unlimited desire that destroys moderation and fills the soul with "imported madness." All lawless desires are awakened; the beast that dreams of incest, murder, any act of folly, takes over the waking life (571b-572b). The tyrant is at once master of others and slave to his own desires — the most wretched of all humans (578c-580a). He lives "like a woman confined to his house" (579b), full of fear, unable to travel freely. A real tyrant is "a slave, compelled to engage in the worst kind of fawning, slavery, and pandering to the worst kind of people" (579d-e).

Three proofs that the just man is happiest:
1. The five character types ranked: philosopher-king (best), timocrat, oligarch, democrat, tyrant (worst)
2. The philosopher has experienced all three pleasures (knowledge, honor, profit) and judges by experience and argument; his judgment is most reliable
3. The philosopher's pleasures fill the soul with what "always is" (Forms); the tyrant's pleasures are shadow-paintings, images of relief from pain, not true pleasures — the tyrant lives **729 times more wretchedly** than the philosopher-king (587e)

**The chimera of the soul** (588b-591b): The soul is like a multiheaded beast (appetites) + a lion (spirit) + a human being (reason), wrapped in a human shell. Justice = the human within tends the lion and tames the beasts. Injustice = the beasts devour reason. "Isn't it necessary for everyone to be ruled by divine reason, preferably within himself and his own, otherwise imposed from without... so that all will be alike and friends?" (590d)

**The city in heaven** (592b): "There is a model of [the ideal city] in heaven, for anyone who wants to look at it and make himself its citizen." The philosopher will take part in the politics of that city — and no other.

### Book X: Poetry, Mimesis, and the Myth of Er
The imitative poet is "third from the truth" — painting appearances of appearances, appealing to the appetitive/emotional part of the soul, not the rational (595a-605c). Poetry "nurtures and waters" the desires, pleasures, and pains "when they ought to wither and be ruled" (606d). The "ancient quarrel between poetry and philosophy" (607b): Plato invites poetry to mount a defense, but unless it can prove itself beneficial, the ban stands.

Proof of the soul's immortality: the soul's own evil (injustice/vice) doesn't destroy it, so the soul must be immortal — this is what it looks like under philosophy's light, not its embodied "barnacle-covered" form (611b-612a).

**The Myth of Er** (614b-621d): The soldier Er dies and revives with a report of the afterlife. The just ascend through heaven; the unjust descend underground for a thousand-year journey, paying their debts tenfold. Tyrants and parricides are permanently barred from ascending — dragged to Tartarus. The **Choice of Lives** (617d-620a): souls choose their next lives by lot but with free will. "Virtue knows no master; each will possess it to a greater or less degree depending on whether he values or disdains it. The responsibility lies with the one who makes the choice; the god has none." The first soul greedily chose the greatest tyranny — and was fated to eat his own children. Odysseus, last to choose, found the life of a private person doing his own work and chose it gladly. Those trained by philosophy and suffering choose wisely; those who came down from heaven untrained (having been virtuous only by habit) choose foolishly.

Final injunction: "Hold to the upward path, practicing justice with reason in every way. That way we'll be friends both to ourselves and to the gods while we remain here on earth and afterwards — we'll receive our rewards." (621c)

## New Right Connections

The Republic is the deepest source for the philosophical ideas the American New Right has absorbed — sometimes consciously (through Strauss), sometimes through the filtered transmission of Bloom, Jaffa, and Claremont, and sometimes inverted (through BAP). The following passages are the most politically live in the New Right context.

### 1. The Cave Allegory as Self-Description (514a-520a)

The Cave is the New Right's primary self-image: the Claremonters are the prisoners who escaped; the American demos are the cave-dwellers who see only shadows (media narratives, progressive ideology, "regime" propaganda). Glenn Ellmers's "The People Are Not the Country" (2021) makes this argument explicitly: "real Americans" (Trump voters) can see clearly; the rest are cave-prisoners. The philosopher who returns to the cave and is mocked — or killed — maps onto the New Right's martyrdom narrative (cancelation, deplatforming, professional destruction for speaking truth).

The Cave also structures the Straussian claim to authority: we understand the text (the political tradition, the founding) at a deeper level than the democratic majority. This claim to superior insight justifies the countermajoritarian political program.

### 2. Knowledge vs. Opinion as Anti-Democratic Epistemology (476a-480a)

Plato's distinction between episteme (knowledge of the Forms) and doxa (opinion about particular things) is the philosophical foundation for the New Right's contempt for democratic deliberation. If the people only have opinion — based on sensation, convention, and media — then their votes do not represent knowledge; they represent preference. Elections measure doxa, not truth. The Claremonters' claim to know what America's founding "truly" means (via Straussian reading of the Declaration) is a claim to episteme over the democratic majority's doxa.

Allan Bloom's *The Closing of the American Mind* (1987) is essentially an extended translation of the Knowledge/Opinion distinction into a critique of American higher education: the university has abandoned the pursuit of truth for the celebration of opinion, and the result is relativism.

### 3. Ship of State as Anti-Democratic Metaphor (488a-489d)

The most vivid anti-democratic image in the Republic. The shipowner (demos) is incompetent; the sailors (politicians) win control by flattery; the true navigator (philosopher) is called useless. Modern democracies choose leaders by flattery and popularity, not by knowledge of governance. [[Michael Anton]]'s "Flight 93 Election" argument — that democratic procedures had produced catastrophically bad leaders and only radical intervention could save the republic — channels this Platonic contempt for democratic choice.

### 4. Democratic Degeneration → Tyranny as Catastrophism (Books VIII-IX)

The degeneration sequence — Aristocracy → Timocracy → Oligarchy → Democracy → Tyranny — is the structural frame behind the New Right's catastrophism: history does not progress; it decays. Plato's analysis of democratic dissolution is cited (or implicitly recycled) across the New Right literature:

- **[[Patrick Deneen]]**'s *Why Liberalism Failed* is a direct translation of the democratic degeneration argument: liberalism's core logic (unlimited freedom, radical individualism, dissolution of all hierarchies and traditions) inevitably destroys itself and paves the way for tyranny.
- **[[Allan Bloom]]**'s *The Closing of the American Mind* opens with the observation that American students "have no heroes" and "know no tragedy" — a diagnosis of a democratic soul that has declared all pleasures equal and lost the capacity for real virtue.
- **[[The Flight 93 Election]]** channels the Platonic idea that democracy, at a certain stage of degeneration, has only two exits: a tyrant who imposes order, or dissolution.
- The specific Platonic diagnoses — "insolence called good breeding; anarchy, freedom; extravagance, magnificence; shamelessness, courage" (560d-561a) — are recycled in New Right critiques of DEI, progressivism, and campus culture.

The **"extreme freedom leads to extreme slavery"** principle (564a) is the most politically live passage in the New Right context. If democratic freedom is taken to its logical conclusion — no constraints, all preferences equal, no natural hierarchy — the result is not a free society but an enslaved one, ruled by the strongest appetite.

### 5. The Noble Lie as Political Mythology (414b-415d)

The Myth of Metals is the most controversial passage in the Republic. The philosopher-kings are to tell citizens a "noble falsehood" (*gennaion pseudos*) about their nature and origin — a founding myth that justifies natural hierarchy. Plato explicitly calls it a lie but defends it as politically necessary.

**Straussian reading**: The Noble Lie demonstrates that all political communities rest on founding myths about origin and natural hierarchy. The political philosopher understands these myths are myths; the demos needs them. This is the basis for Strauss's claim that the philosopher has a different relationship to political rhetoric than ordinary citizens — what Strauss called "gentlemanly" deception.

**New Right application**: The New Right's own founding myth — "America is a nation rooted in natural right, and the progressive left has corrupted it" — has a similar structure to the Myth of Metals. It provides a hierarchical account of political legitimacy (those who understand the founding = gold; those who don't = iron/bronze), and it is deployed politically rather than philosophically demonstrated. The Claremont reading of the Declaration of Independence (*all men are created equal* → natural right hierarchy, not democratic equality) is a Noble Lie in precisely the Platonic sense: a political myth that justifies a specific ruling claim.

### 6. The Philosopher-King and Claims to Political Authority (473c-d, 502a-b)

"Until philosophers rule as kings... cities will have no rest from evils." The Claremonters' claim to authority — that they alone understand what the founding truly means, what natural right requires, what the Constitution demands — is a philosopher-king claim in secular dress. They are not elected; they claim to know. The Claremont Institute's entire institutional project (fellowships, publications, the Publius fellows program) is a project of producing philosopher-kings — people who understand the *true* political order at a level the democratic majority cannot.

Note the Platonic irony: Plato says philosophers are "kings not eager to rule" (520d) — the best governors precisely because they do not want power. The New Right's intense drive for power inverts this requirement.

### 7. Culture Precedes Politics — The Damon Thesis (424b-c)

"Musical modes are never changed without change to the most important of a city's laws." Damon's thesis — that changes in musical modes always accompany political changes — is the ancient source of the New Right's "Ideas First" or "culture war first" strategy. [[Sam Francis]]'s Gramscian analysis (the right must capture cultural institutions before it can hold political power), [[Longmarcherism]], and [[Christopher Rufo]]'s explicit campaign to capture universities and K-12 education all implement this Platonic insight.

Poetry (Book X) extends the argument: imitative culture that appeals to the emotional/appetitive parts strengthens the wrong parts of the soul and corrupts political judgment. The New Right's claim that progressive media, Hollywood, and universities do exactly what Plato says the imitative poets do — appeal to appetite and emotion, weaken reason, corrupt the demos — is a direct application of this argument.

### 8. BAP's Inverted Platonism: Thymos against Logos

[[Costin Alamariu]]'s Yale dissertation was titled "The Problem of Tyranny and Philosophy in the Thought of Plato and Nietzsche" — making the Republic-Nietzsche synthesis the explicit academic foundation of his intellectual project. BAP's reading of Plato is not straightforwardly Straussian; it is an *inversion*.

**Where Strauss valorizes logos** (reason, the philosopher's knowledge), **BAP valorizes thymos** (the spirited part — the warrior's desire for victory and honor). BAP's "Bronze Age" ideal is the timocratic man — the spirited, warrior soul that Plato ranks *second* among constitutional types (547d-548b). BAP's explicit celebration of male vitality, athletic bodies, and will-to-domination is a rehabilitation of the timocratic soul that Plato treats as the *first stage of degeneration* from the ideal.

The **"beast within"** passage (571b-572b) — the recognition that in sleep, when reason slumbers, a dangerous and savage part "will commit any foul murder, will commit incest with a mother, will eat any food" — is what BAP's work addresses in an inverted register. Where Plato seeks to discipline the beast through education and law, BAP celebrates it as the source of vitality and exhorts its readers to let it breathe.

The **tyrannical-erotic drive** (573a) — erotic love as "a great winged drone" that occupies the citadel of the soul — is, for BAP, not the soul's worst enemy but its most interesting inhabitant. *Bronze Age Mindset* is written from the perspective of the tyrannical-erotic soul that Plato would banish or tame. BAP's self-published dissertation (*Selective Breeding and the Birth of Philosophy*, 2023) argued that **philosophy and tyranny are indelibly connected — not as antagonists but as kindred**.

This is a coherent (if alarming) reading of the Republic: Socrates is himself a figure of transgressive vitality; the dialogue depicts the very thing it ostensibly condemns. BAP follows this thread to its Nietzschean conclusion: the philosopher is a kind of tyrant of the soul.

### 9. Common Good and Natural Hierarchy: Integralist Connections (590d, 414b-415d)

"Isn't it necessary for everyone to be ruled by divine reason, preferably within himself and his own, otherwise imposed from without, so that all will be alike and friends?" (590d) This passage is the ancient source of [[Adrian Vermeule]]'s [[Common Good Constitutionalism]]: the law is external reason for those who cannot rule themselves by internal reason. The state has an obligation to impose good order on citizens who lack the philosophical training to govern their own souls.

The Platonic structure — natural hierarchy justified by a founding myth (Noble Lie) + philosopher-king authority + law as the ally of reason — maps almost exactly onto Catholic integralism's structure: natural law hierarchy + revealed theology + the Church's authority to shape civil law.

Vermeule's explicit citation of classical natural law (Aquinas, not Plato) conceals the deeper Platonic structure. The Republic provides the philosophical architecture that Aquinas later Christianized.

### 10. The "City in Heaven" and the Impossibility of the Ideal City (592b)

"There is a model of [the ideal city] in heaven, for anyone who wants to look at it and to make himself its citizen on the strength of what he sees. It makes no difference whether it is or ever will be somewhere, for he would take part in the practical affairs of that city and no other."

This passage is crucial for the **Straussian reading** of the Republic and for understanding what the New Right actually takes from it. Strauss argued that the *Republic*'s ideal city is *not a political program* but a *theoretical standard* — the ideal city demonstrates the limits of politics, not a blueprint for action. The good city exists as a heavenly model; the philosopher's task is to orient himself by it, not to impose it.

The New Right's relationship to this insight is contradictory: the Claremonters claim Strauss's philosophical authority while pursuing a very un-Platonic hunger for political power. If the ideal city only exists in heaven, then the philosopher-king's proper role is private (tend your soul; leave politics to the timocrats who want it) — which is precisely what the New Right refuses to do.

## Passages the New Right Ignores

The Republic contains several passages that the New Right's selective Platonism quietly elides:

- **Guardian communism** (415c-417b, 457c-462e): Guardians/philosopher-kings are explicitly prohibited from private property, private marriages, private money, or private households. The New Right's alliance with corporate capital and its celebration of private wealth is precisely what Plato's guardians are forbidden.
- **Women as guardians** (451c-457b): Women with guardian natures should receive exactly the same education and serve as guardians. "On the whole, one sex is much superior to the other in pretty well everything, although many women are better than many men in many things" (455d-e). BAP's misogyny requires ignoring this passage entirely.
- **Infanticide and selective breeding** (460b-461d): Rulers are to arrange breeding secretly; children of inferior parents are to be "hidden away, in some appropriate manner, that must be kept secret" — a provision for infanticide/exposure that the New Right would find politically inconvenient.
- **The philosopher does NOT want to rule** (519d-521b): The ideal ruler governs reluctantly, as a duty, not from ambition. The New Right's intense, aggressive pursuit of power inverts the Platonic requirement.
- **729 times** (587e): The philosopher's life is 729 times more pleasant than the tyrant's. If this is taken seriously, the aspiring tyrant-strongman is by definition living the worst possible life.

## Strauss's Readings: What Straussians Debate

The Republic has been the central text in Straussian scholarship, with major disputes:

**The esoteric reading**: Strauss argued that the Republic's most radical proposals (guardian communism, philosopher-kings, the Noble Lie) are deliberately absurd — Plato is *showing the impossibility* of the ideal city, not prescribing it. The ideal city is a reductio of the political project: if you push politics to its logical extreme, you get the *Republic*'s city — which requires eliminating the family, private property, and the very conditions of private life. The dialogue is a warning, not a blueprint. This is why the city only "exists in heaven" (592b).

**The literal/activist reading** (West Coast Straussians): The Republic's natural right framework (there are knowable standards of justice) is politically deployable; the ideal city shows what complete justice would look like; the American founding is an approximation of this standard; the task of political philosophy is to hold the regime to the standard.

**The key disputed question**: Does Plato endorse the philosopher-king as a political program, or does he demonstrate that such a program is impossible and that philosophy must remain separate from politics? The Straussian left and right split on this question, with significant consequences for how they approach political engagement.
