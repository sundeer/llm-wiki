# António de Oliveira Salazar (1889–1970)

**Portuguese economist; Coimbra finance minister 1928, prime minister 1932–1968; founder and leader of the *Estado Novo* (New State) corporatist authoritarian regime (1933–1974); Dean Acheson called him "the nearest approach in our time to Plato's philosopher-king"; the wiki's principal *corporatist*-authoritarian reference for [[Gladden Pappin|Pappin]] / [[Adrian Vermeule|Vermeule]]-style contemporary postliberal political-form thinking.**

---

## Why He Matters Here

Where [[Francisco Franco|Franco]] is the wiki's "Throne and Altar" figure, Salazar is the *corporatist administrative state* figure. The two regimes were both Catholic, both authoritarian, both anti-communist, and both admired by the same postwar [[National Review|*NR*]] circle — but their structures differed in ways that map onto contemporary New Right divides:

- **Franco** = military strongman + Catholic concordat + anti-communist crusader → admired by the Throne-and-Altar Catholic right (Buckley, Bozell)
- **Salazar** = Catholic technocratic professor + corporatist representation + administrative-state primacy + tactical neutralism → admired today by the *administrative-state-capture* postliberal right (Vermeule, Pappin)

Salazar's relevance to the contemporary wiki is the **corporatism** thread. Pappin's "Corporatism for the Twenty-First Century" (2020) and Helen Andrews's 2021 *First Things* essay are explicit about porting *Estado Novo* corporatism into a 21st-century U.S. setting.

## Brief Biography

- Born Vimieiro, Portugal, 1889. Trained for the Catholic priesthood (took minor orders); diverted to economics at Coimbra.
- 1928: appointed Finance Minister with extraordinary powers after the 1926 military coup. Balanced the budget within a year — the basis for his subsequent reputation as a technocratic miracle-worker.
- 1932: appointed Prime Minister. 1933 constitution established the *Estado Novo* — corporatist, authoritarian, Catholic-aligned, single-party (*União Nacional*).
- WWII: maintained Portuguese neutrality but tilted toward the Allies on bases (Azores). Allowed Allied use of Lajes Field 1943.
- Post-WWII: Portugal admitted to UN (1955), founding NATO member (1949), joined EFTA (1960). The regime survived democratization elsewhere in Western Europe by tying itself to the Cold War Atlantic alliance.
- 1961: outbreak of the Portuguese Colonial War (Angola, Mozambique, Guinea-Bissau). The colonial wars consumed ~40% of state spending by the late 1960s.
- 1968: incapacitated by stroke. Marcelo Caetano took over but Salazar was not informed and continued to believe he ruled until his 1970 death.
- 1974: the regime fell to the Carnation Revolution.

## *Estado Novo* — Structural Features Relevant to the Wiki

1. **Corporatist representation.** The *Câmara Corporativa* organized representation by sector (employer/employee guilds, professions, religious bodies) rather than territorial constituencies. Theoretically subordinated electoral politics to functional representation; in practice consolidated single-party rule but provided the *form* contemporary postliberals are reviving.
2. **Catholic Concordat (1940).** Did not establish Catholicism but gave it privileged status. Less integralist than Franco's Spain; more "social-Catholic" than "altar-and-throne."
3. **Bureaucratic continuity.** Salazar's administrative state was technocratic and durable. [[Adrian Vermeule]]: "Salazar's Portugal as a country where bureaucracy has flourished in an illiberal regime."
4. **PIDE secret police.** The regime's organ of suppression. The wiki notes this for completeness; contemporary postliberal admiration carefully does not engage with the PIDE record.

## The American Conservative Reception

### Period 1 (1950s–60s)

Per [[Iberian Vistas]]:

- **Dean Acheson (1962)** — Truman's Secretary of State, lifelong Democrat, met Salazar in Lisbon 1952: "the nearest approach in our time to Plato's philosopher-king… I had never spent a more revealing hour, and had met a man unique in his time, the possessor of a rare mind and even rarer charm." Acheson's encomium is the wiki's load-bearing fact that *moderate-establishment* Cold War America also took Salazar seriously — not just the *NR* right.
- **Buckley (1958, *NR*).** "We do not despise Salazar for using force to keep his highly benevolent regime in power."
- **Buckley (1958, Hunter College debate).** "There are many more allusions, on college campuses, to the fact that Salazar governs Portugal undemocratically than to the fact that he governs it well."
- **Salazar in *NR* (1956).** "Be resolved to fight" — Salazar in his own voice in *NR*.
- **James Burnham, Erik von Kuehnelt-Leddihn, Thomas Molnar.** Serial defenses of Portuguese colonialism in Angola and Mozambique. The wiki reads this as *NR*-circle anti-decolonization — civilization-defense rhetoric *materially structured* as defense of European imperialism in Africa.
- **Catholicism.org / *Triumph*.** Bozell's *Triumph* magazine (1966–76) supplied the most ardent American-Catholic Salazar admiration.

### Period 2 (2010s–present)

- **Tom Gallagher's *Salazar: The Dictator Who Refused to Die* (2020).** Sympathetic biography by a Scottish political scientist; the *trigger* for the contemporary American revival.
- **Michael Warren Davis** (*The American Conservative*, 2021, "Waiting for Salazar"): "if liberalism *is* off the table, the only alternative to a tyrant like Lenin or Hitler may be a man like Salazar: a paternalistic traditionalist, a philosopher king."
- **Helen Andrews** (*First Things*, 2021): "The Portuguese had Salazar single-handedly mediating among society's various interests. They also had a shared idea of the common good and a Catholic moral vocabulary to which competing parties could appeal. Without these external constraints, rule by elites is not corporatism but oligarchy."
- **Christopher Roach**, "The Salazar Option" (*American Greatness*, 2021): in the face of revolutionary leftism, "use of State power at the service of a counterrevolution"; reject the [[Rod Dreher|Dreher]] "Benedict option."
- **Adrian Vermeule.** Cites Salazar's Portugal as exemplary of bureaucracy flourishing in an illiberal regime. Foundational for [[Common Good Constitutionalism]]'s administrative-state focus.
- **Gladden Pappin.** "Corporatism for the Twenty-First Century" (2020): defends "a state-led program to establish the corporate bodies of society and bring them to negotiations." This is *Estado Novo* corporatism in 21st-century vocabulary.
- **Charles Haywood.** "For post-liberals in particular, Salazar is necessarily interesting, since he is one of the few twentieth-century examples of a long-lived Right regime that successfully opposed the corrosion of Enlightenment liberalism."

## Structural Place in the Wiki

Salazar's *Estado Novo* is the **regime-type referent** for the contemporary postliberal political-form imagination — particularly Pappin's corporatism and Vermeule's administrative-state primacy. The wiki's structural reading: where critics of the postliberal current ask "*what does the postliberal regime look like?*", *Estado Novo* Portugal is the most direct historical analogue — a Catholic, corporatist, technocratic, authoritarian, anti-communist administrative state. (See [[Iberian Vistas]] for the academic version.)

The same caveat applies as to Franco: the *constituency* for a Catholic corporatist transformation is shrinking. The intellectual revival is real; the political viability is limited (Hawley 2024). Salazar belongs in the wiki as a diagnostic indicator of postliberal *intellectual* ambition.
