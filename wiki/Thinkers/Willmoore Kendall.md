# Willmoore Kendall (1909–1967)

**Yale political theorist (1947–1961); Catholic convert; [[William F. Buckley Jr.|Buckley]]'s undergraduate teacher and a founding senior editor of [[National Review|*NR*]] (1955); the wiki's principal *intellectual* point of origin for the populist-majoritarian, anti-liberal strain of postwar American conservatism that runs through [[L. Brent Bozell Jr.|Bozell]] to contemporary [[Postliberalism|postliberalism]]; lived in Franco's Spain for several years.**

---

## Why He Matters Here

Kendall is the wiki's *missing first link* between European Catholic political theory and the American conservative movement. He was the figure who taught Buckley to read [[Plato]], Hobbes, Locke, and Rousseau as *political* rather than *philosophical* texts — and who supplied Buckley's anti-liberalism its theoretical scaffolding. *Triumph* magazine, the [[Bozell]]-era Catholic-traditionalist publication, was substantially a Kendall-influenced project. [[George W. Carey]]'s *Basic Symbols of the American Political Tradition* (1970, posthumously co-authored with Kendall) is the underlying anti-Lockean reading of American founding documents that the [[The Claremonters|Claremonters]] would later overcome with their counter-Lockean Lincolnian-Declarationist reading.

Kendall is also the underdiagnosed *populist-majoritarian* current in postwar conservatism. His political theory held that the American constitutional order is fundamentally about *deliberative legislative supremacy* exercised by a "virtuous people" — a position that prefigures contemporary right-populist majoritarianism while remaining philosophically grounded in classical political theory.

---

## Career

- Born Konawa, Oklahoma, 1909; Methodist minister's son; Rhodes Scholar (Oxford, Pembroke College, with [[Eric Voegelin]] and Leo Strauss-adjacent figures).
- Brief journalist, then Yale political science PhD (1941).
- **OSS during WWII.** Latin America operations — first encounter with Hispanic political culture.
- **Yale faculty 1947–1961.** Tenured but isolated; Yale colleagues found him personally difficult; Buckley described their disputes as legendary. Trained Buckley undergraduate, taught a generation of conservative intellectuals including [[Brent Bozell|Bozell]], [[Garry Wills]], and [[M.E. Bradford]] (briefly).
- **Convert to Catholicism, c. 1956.** Per [[Iberian Vistas|Zúquete & Hawley]], the conversion was part of the *NR* circle's overrepresented Catholic gravitational pull.
- **1955: founding senior editor of *NR*.** Departed editorial role acrimoniously by the early 1960s but maintained relations.
- **Lived in Franco's Spain** for an extended period in the late 1950s/early 1960s. Bozell joined him; the Iberian Catholic context shaped both men's later work.
- **1963: bought out of Yale for $42,500** — the legendary terms of his Yale departure. Joined the brand-new University of Dallas as founding chair of the Politics department, which became the principal redoubt of [[paleoconservatism|paleoconservative]] political theory under his and Bradford's tenure.
- Died Dallas, 1967, age 58.

## Key Ideas

### Deliberative Legislative Supremacy

Kendall's central political-theoretical claim: the American constitutional order is *not* fundamentally Lockean (rights-individualist) but *legislative-majoritarian*. Congress, deliberating in pursuit of the public good, is the constitutional center. The Bill of Rights is constraint, not foundation. The judiciary should defer.

This view has two New Right afterlives:

1. **The Bradford/paleoconservative line.** Kendall trained M.E. Bradford at Dallas; Bradford's anti-Lincoln, anti-Declarationist constitutional reading is structurally Kendallian. See [[M.E. Bradford]]; [[Beyond Originalism]] (Kersch).
2. **The contemporary anti-judicial-supremacy strain.** [[Adrian Vermeule|Vermeule]]'s [[Common Good Constitutionalism]] and the broader postliberal preference for legislative or executive rather than judicial action proceed from a Kendallian premise (though Vermeule does not directly cite him).

### *Basic Symbols of the American Political Tradition* (1970)

Co-authored with [[George W. Carey]] (Kendall's student), published posthumously. Argues that the American tradition is a *Christian republican* tradition founded in the Mayflower Compact, not a *liberal* tradition founded in the Declaration of Independence. The Declaration's "all men are created equal" is read as a *legal* (not metaphysical-egalitarian) claim about colonial separation from Britain.

This is the *paleoconservative* reading of the founding the [[Harry V. Jaffa|Jaffa]] / Claremont reading was constructed against. The wiki's Bradford-Jaffa axis (see [[Beyond Originalism]]) has Kendall as Bradford's intellectual godfather.

### Anti-Liberalism via Rousseau

Kendall taught a *populist-majoritarian* reading of Rousseau against Locke that rejected liberal-individualist political theory. His students at Yale and Dallas absorbed this; it is one of the underdiagnosed sources of populist anti-liberalism in postwar American conservatism.

## Iberian Connection

Per [[Iberian Vistas]]: Kendall lived in Franco's Spain in the late 1950s and early 1960s. Bozell joined him there. Both men's anti-fusionist Catholic-traditionalist trajectories were materially shaped by their Iberian residence; both effectively rejected Buckley/Meyer fusionism in favor of substantively Catholic political theory after their Spanish period. Kendall is a primary case for the wiki's Iberian-admiration thread as a *formative* (not merely rhetorical) influence on postwar conservatism.

## What the Wiki Inherits

- Bozell's [[Triumph]] (1966–1976) is in significant part a Kendallian project: Catholic populist majoritarianism as the right alternative to fusionism.
- The University of Dallas as a paleoconservative redoubt (Bradford et al.) is Kendall's institutional legacy.
- [[Patrick Deneen]]'s anti-Lockean reading of American founding documents (in [[Why Liberalism Failed]] and [[Regime Change]]) is structurally Kendallian, transmitted via [[Wilson Carey McWilliams]] (Deneen's mentor at Rutgers; trained in part by Kendall's intellectual generation) and via Notre Dame Catholic political theory.
- The contemporary [[Postliberalism|postliberal]] preference for legislative/executive over judicial action has a Kendallian root.
