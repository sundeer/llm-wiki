# John Birch Society

**Far-right anti-communist organization founded 1958 by retired candy manufacturer [[Robert Welch]]; named after U.S. Army Capt. John M. Birch (intelligence officer killed by Chinese communists in August 1945; Welch considered him the Cold War's first casualty); peaked at ~100,000 members in the early 1960s; major node of conspiratorial, racially reactionary, anti-civil-rights politics on the postwar American right; the *purported* expulsion of the JBS by [[William F. Buckley Jr.]] is one of the conservative movement's foundational origin myths and a focal point of the wiki's [[Counterrevolution]] thesis.**

---

## What It Was

Founded by Robert Welch with a two-day presentation to a small group of business executives in December 1958. Funded initially by anti–New Deal businessmen. The organization grew rapidly through the early 1960s into "a major faction on the American right, especially in California" (Trickey), with hundreds of local chapters that encouraged members to run for local office and to support far-right campaigns. JBS published the magazine *American Opinion*, headquartered in Belmont, Massachusetts.

### Core Politics
- **Anti-communism, escalated to conspiracism.** Welch's 1961 claim that the U.S. government was "50 to 70 percent" communist-controlled. Welch's screed *The Politician* called Eisenhower "a dedicated, conscious agent of the Communist conspiracy."
- **Anti–civil rights.** JBS smeared Martin Luther King Jr. as a communist. *American Opinion* described Selma protesters as "a horde of termites." Welch attributed Black southerners' civil-rights activism "entirely" to communist agitation.
- **Anti–institutional.** Billboards across the country called for impeaching Chief Justice Earl Warren over the Supreme Court's civil-liberties and desegregation rulings. Pushed U.S. exit from the United Nations, abolition of the income tax, preservation of the House Un-American Activities Committee.
- **Conspiracism as method.** Fluoridation as a communist plot; speculation that Sen. Robert Taft's cancer was "induced by a radium tube planted in the upholstery of his Senate seat."
- **Antisemitic currents.** Some members urged the Society toward more openly antisemitic positions; founding member Revilo Oliver was a notorious antisemitic conspiracist who maintained close ties with Buckley.

### Tactics
- Mass-mail flooding of congressional offices.
- A national magazine and pamphlet network.
- Local chapter organizing aimed at downballot Republican primaries.
- Front groups, including the "Committee Against Summit Entanglements" opposing the 1959 Eisenhower-Khrushchev summit.

## The Buckley Episode (1961–1965)

The JBS is best known to political history as the case study in mainstream-conservative "boundary policing." The standard narrative ([[Long before QAnon, Ronald Reagan and the GOP purged John Birch extremists from the party]]):

1. **1962 Palm Beach meeting.** Buckley, [[Russell Kirk]], and [[Barry Goldwater]] meet at the Breakers; agree on a strategy of attacking Welch personally while preserving the rank-and-file.
2. **April 1961 / February 1962** *National Review* editorials: Welch's views "far removed from common sense"; Goldwater's accompanying letter denies Welch represents the membership.
3. **August–October 1965**: Buckley's syndicated column attacks *American Opinion*'s civil-rights smears; subscriber backlash; Buckley doubles down. Reagan declares "I am not a member" (September 1965); Dirksen and Ford jointly denounce the JBS; *NR* runs a 14-page anti-Bircher special. Goldwater calls for conservatives to resign from the JBS unless Welch does. Bogus's biography: "no fig leaf of respectability remained… steep decline."

### The 1965 Membership Surge (Holden 2024)

Per [[The Republican National Convention That Shocked the Country|Holden]]: while Buckley was conducting his October 1965 *NR* anti-Bircher campaign, JBS membership was *flourishing*. The November 1965 *NYT* reported the surge; the JBS publicity director added Selma sheriff James G. Clark — who had directed the violence on John Lewis at the Edmund Pettus Bridge that March — to its lecture circuit. The October 1965 ADL reported a sharp rise in KKK membership outside the South in the same month.

The Holden synthesis is wiki-significant: the *visible* mainstream-conservative repudiation of the JBS coincided with the *operational* expansion of the JBS and the parallel KKK surge. The 1965 NYC Fifth Avenue pro-war march (Buckley alongside longshoremen and Medal of Honor winners; "Bomb Hanoi" floats; antiwar protestors beaten unconscious) is the same coalition arc. The wiki reads this as confirmation that the *temperamental* and *operational* Bircher-adjacent right was not contracting in 1965 — the brand-management performance ran parallel to a substantively larger movement.

The revisionist narrative ([[Debunking a Longstanding Myth About William F. Buckley]] / Dallek 2023, citing Schlozman and Rosenfeld):

- The "excommunication" was structurally limited to Welch personally. Buckley wrote to Birch founder T. Coleman Andrews: "I don't think in my life I have made a single unfavorable reference to any members of the John Birch Society."
- Many *NR* subscribers also subscribed to *American Opinion*; subscriber overlap persisted.
- Buckley's 1965 NYC Conservative Party mayoral run drew Birch and fringe support.
- Buckley maintained personal and professional ties to extreme Birch leaders, including Revilo Oliver.
- Goldwater's 1964 campaign welcomed the rank-and-file and many of their ideas; "extremism in the defense of liberty is no vice."
- The schism was ideologically narrow: the Birchers were sacrificed *as a brand*, not as a politics.

The wiki sides with the Dallek revisionism. See [[Counterrevolution]].

## Decline and Survival

By the late 1960s the JBS's national influence was much diminished — Welch's late writings (e.g., the late-1960s "Insiders" theory blaming a banking elite) made the Society easier to marginalize, and the rise of more polished movement institutions (think tanks, *NR*'s expanded reach, [[Heritage Foundation]] from 1973) absorbed the activist base while disclaiming the brand. The organization continues to exist (as of the 2020s) as a marginal right-wing nonprofit; its substantive politics, however, did not so much die as diffuse — into [[Paleoconservatism]], the Buchanan campaigns, the militia movement, and ultimately the conspiracist substrate that the [[MAGA New Right]] mobilized.

## Why It Matters for the Wiki

The JBS is the **first major test case** for the wiki's central question: was American conservatism ever genuinely separate from the conspiracist, racially reactionary, restorationist far right, or has the movement's mainstream always been continuous with its fringe and merely managed the optics?

[[Joshua Tait]]'s [[Counterrevolution]] thesis says continuous; Dallek's *Birchers* says continuous; the standard "Buckley purged them" narrative ([[Russell Kirk]]'s recollections, Trickey 2021, Bogus 2013) says discontinuous. The wiki treats the Bircher case as the strongest single empirical lever for the continuity reading. The post-2016 mainstreaming of conspiracism, racial demographic anxiety, anti-institutionalism, and "stolen country" rhetoric is then legible not as a *return* of the repressed but as the *resurfacing* of what was always there — once the brand-management consensus collapsed.
