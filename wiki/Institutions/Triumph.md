# Triumph (1966–1976)

**Traditionalist Catholic political-and-religious magazine; founded and edited by [[L. Brent Bozell Jr.]]; explicit Catholic-theocratic alternative to *National Review*-style fusionism; the wiki's principal *historical* example of the Catholic-integralist political program inside postwar American conservatism — and the institutional-form precursor of contemporary [[Postliberal Order]] and the [[First Things]] "strong gods" current.**

---

## Why It Matters Here

*Triumph* is the wiki's missing institutional bridge between the postwar Catholic *NR* circle and contemporary [[Catholic Integralism]]. Three reasons:

1. **The substantive program.** *Triumph* held what would become the [[Adrian Vermeule|Vermeule]]-[[Patrick Deneen|Deneen]]-[[Sohrab Ahmari|Ahmari]] position six decades early: politics aims at the substantive good (Christian civilization), not at the procedural protection of liberty; the state exists to facilitate the salvation of souls; American constitutionalism's neutrality among ends is a category error.
2. **The Iberian connection.** Per [[Iberian Vistas]] (Zúquete & Hawley), *Triumph* was the most ardent American venue for Salazar admiration. [[Willmoore Kendall|Kendall]] and Bozell had lived in Franco's Spain; *Triumph* was structurally a Spanish-Catholic-traditionalism transplant project.
3. **The institutional fate.** *Triumph* failed — folded in 1976, never reached significant circulation, marginalized its founder. The institutional failure is why contemporary postliberals can credibly claim to be doing something *new*: the prior attempt was so marginal it left no living institutional memory.

---

## History

- **Founded 1966** by Bozell with substantial Catholic-traditionalist support. Its principal financial backer was the small *Triumph* circle of donors; the magazine never achieved commercial viability.
- **Editorial position.** Anti-Vatican-II (which had just closed in 1965); anti-fusionist; pro-Salazar; pro-Franco; opposed to American Cold War realpolitik when it required cooperation with non-Catholic powers; in favor of *substantive* Christian-civilization politics.
- **Operational politics.** Bozell led the *Triumph* staff in a 1970 raid on a Washington, D.C. abortion clinic — physically entering the clinic, praying, confronting providers. The action prefigures Operation Rescue (1986–) and the contemporary direct-action religious-Right register more than it resembles anything in the *NR*-circle of the time.
- **Decline.** Bozell's mental health declined through the 1970s. The magazine's political prescriptions became more openly theocratic and the editorial circle more isolated. *Triumph* folded in 1976; Bozell never produced another comparable institution.

## Editorial-Content Map

*Triumph* covered a recognizable cluster of topics that the wiki tracks across contemporary postliberal venues:

- **Catholic Social Teaching.** Subsidiarity, solidarity, the common good — read as substantive moral commitments rather than procedural framings. The vocabulary [[Gladden Pappin]] now uses in *American Affairs* and [[Postliberal Order]] is structurally *Triumph* vocabulary.
- **Iberian admiration.** Salazar covered repeatedly; Franco regularly. The *Estado Novo* corporatist model framed as the institutional alternative to liberal democracy. (See [[António Salazar]], [[Iberian Vistas]].)
- **Anti-Lockean American constitutional theory.** The Kendall populist-majoritarian reading that became the paleoconservative anti-Declarationist line.
- **Critique of fusionism.** Bozell's position — that fusionism is the disease, not the cure — was *Triumph*'s editorial spine.
- **Abortion as central political issue.** Long before *Roe v. Wade* (1973), *Triumph* was treating abortion as the defining political-moral question of the era, in advance of the broader Christian Right's mobilization.

---

## Comparison with *National Review*

*Triumph* and *NR* were both Catholic-disproportionate, both anti-communist, both in personal contact (Bozell was Buckley's brother-in-law). The substantive break was on fusionism: *NR* held the Meyer fusionist line; *Triumph* rejected it. The political break was on tactics: *NR* worked through the Republican Party; *Triumph* held that Catholic-theocratic politics could not be vehicle'd through American party politics and required a parallel-institution strategy.

The contemporary [[Postliberal Order]] / *American Affairs* / *First Things* triad is *NR*-and-*Triumph* simultaneously — using mainstream-conservative venues to articulate a substantively *Triumph*-aligned program.

## Wiki Reading

The wiki preserves *Triumph* as the case study in *failed* American Catholic-theocratic politics. The 1966–1976 attempt was unviable because:

- The Cold War cement still held — anti-communism kept the fusionist alliance functional and made the *Triumph* break appear unnecessary
- Vatican II had just liberalized the Catholic Church — Bozell's traditionalism was running against the institutional Church's self-understanding
- The American Catholic political constituency was overwhelmingly Democratic-blue-collar in 1970 — the structural base for Catholic-theocratic Republican politics did not yet exist

Contemporary postliberals enjoy none of these obstacles in the same form. The wiki's reading is that the *Triumph* program was *substantively* correct about its own moment but *operationally* impossible; the contemporary version may be operationally more viable but is running with a substantially shrunken religious base. (Compare Hawley's empirical-electoral cautions in [[Iberian Vistas]].)
