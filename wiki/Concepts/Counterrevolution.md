# Counterrevolution

**The claim, advanced by historians of conservatism like [[Joshua Tait]], that the American Conservative Movement has always been functionally counterrevolutionary — aiming to restore a prior political and cultural order — rather than genuinely conservative in the Burkean sense of preserving the present; the concept frames the continuity between 1950s movement conservatism, paleoconservatism, and the MAGA New Right**

---

## The Conservative-Counterrevolutionary Distinction

Genuine Burkean conservatism, as theorized by [[Russell Kirk]] and Michael Oakeshott, is a *disposition* — an attachment to what exists, a preference for the familiar, a deep suspicion of deliberate political engineering. It accepts losses. It accommodates change. It is, in Oakeshott's phrase, "unadventurous."

A *counterrevolution* is something different: a deliberate attempt to reverse changes that have already occurred and restore a prior state. It requires active, often coercive, political mobilization. It is not conservative; it is restorationist.

Historian Joshua Tait's thesis (2024): the American Conservative Movement has always sailed under the conservative flag while being functionally counterrevolutionary. The *National Review* prospectus (1955) called the New Deal a "total revolution" — but this very framing made genuine conservatism impossible. If the republic was already lost, what was there to conserve?

---

## The Mechanism: Catastrophism

The engine that converts conservatism into counterrevolution is **catastrophism** — the insistence that the hour is late, the republic is dying or already dead, and normal political norms no longer apply.

[[The Flight 93 Election]] (Michael Anton, 2016) is the purest expression of this logic: vote Trump or the plane crashes. If you believe the plane is already going down, storming the cockpit isn't counterrevolution — it's survival. The catastrophist frame *licenses* whatever means are necessary.

The Claremont Institute has been catastrophism's institutional home. As [[Thomas Klingenstein]] framed it: "If it's true that the country is as divided as we think it is, and if the situation is as dire... if you're actually in a war, even if it's a cold war, you behave differently." The catastrophist premise generates the wartime conclusion.

## The 1964 RNC as First Counterrevolutionary Moment

The wiki treats the [[1964 Republican National Convention]] as the *first* visible counterrevolutionary moment in postwar Republican politics — *not* the 1955 founding of *NR* (an intellectual project), nor the [[The Conscience of a Conservative|*Conscience of a Conservative*]] publication (a manifesto), but the convention itself, where the temperamental signature appears in operational form: rage at media (Eisenhower attacking the press; "broadcast from Moscow" hostility to NBC), refusal to disavow extremists (the rejection of Hugh Scott's anti-extremism plank, the booing of Rockefeller), the conspiracist frame (Schlafly's "secret kingmakers"), and the catastrophist self-understanding ("Glorious Disaster"). The *temperament* is what is conserved across decades; the policy content varies.

[[Jackie Robinson]]'s 1964 diagnosis is, in this regard, the wiki's earliest articulation of the Counterrevolution thesis: "If Goldwater has been defeated, but Goldwaterism remains triumphant in GOP councils, America faces a difficult future." See [[How Jackie Robinson Helped Defeat a Trump-Like Candidate]].

[[The Republican National Convention That Shocked the Country|Holden 2024]] traces the transmission directly: 1964 Cow Palace → 1965 NYC pro-war street violence → 1970 Hard Hat Riot → Newt Gingrich's 1980s–90s GOPAC vocabulary ("sick, pathetic, lie, anti-flag, traitors") → 2016 Trump rallies → Oath Keepers/Three Percenters/Proud Boys.

## The Limbaugh Vernacular

[[Rush Limbaugh|Limbaugh]]'s September 7, 2016 broadcast (per [[The Shaming of the Never Trumpers]]) supplies the cleanest single statement of the Counterrevolution thesis in popular register: "Conservatism no longer applies. We're way past that." Limbaugh's gloss on Anton's [[The Flight 93 Election]] makes explicit what Anton's piece states only obliquely: that the existing institutions of mainstream conservatism are not merely inadequate but *complicit*, and that the Counterrevolution requires repudiating them along with the broader liberal order. The wiki reads this as the *vernacular* version of Tait's later academic argument.

## The Iberian Continuity

Per [[Iberian Vistas]] (Zúquete & Hawley, 2025): the postwar [[National Review|*NR*]] circle's admiration for [[Francisco Franco|Franco]] and [[António Salazar|Salazar]] — and the contemporary postliberal revival of the same admiration — is itself a counterrevolutionary thread. [[Thomas Molnar]]'s *The Counter-Revolution* (1969) supplies a self-conscious Catholic articulation: "counter-revolution" as positive substantive politics aimed at restoring proper hierarchical order. The contemporary American postliberal version (Pappin's corporatism, Vermeule's administrative-state capture, Haywood's "Foundationalism") is recognizably the same project at a 60-year remove.

## The Buckley/Birch Case Study (1961–1965)

The [[John Birch Society]] episode is the wiki's strongest empirical lever for Tait's continuity thesis. The standard narrative ([[Long before QAnon, Ronald Reagan and the GOP purged John Birch extremists from the party]], Trickey 2021) is that [[William F. Buckley Jr.]] disciplined the conspiratorial right out of the conservative movement through his 1961–1965 *National Review* editorials. The revisionist account ([[Debunking a Longstanding Myth About William F. Buckley]], Dallek 2023, citing Schlozman and Rosenfeld) is that Buckley's "excommunication" was a *cordon sanitaire* drawn around [[Robert Welch]] personally while the rank-and-file, the subscriber overlap, and the substantive Bircher politics — anti–civil rights, anti-UN nationalism, anti-institutional conspiracism, racial demographic anxiety — remained inside the conservative tent.

Buckley's own letter to Birch founder T. Coleman Andrews makes the point flatly: "I don't think in my life I have made a single unfavorable reference to any members of the John Birch Society."

The implication: there was never a successful purge. The post-2016 mainstreaming of conspiracism, racial demographic anxiety, and "stolen country" rhetoric is not a *return* of what was once expelled; it is the *resurfacing* of what was always there once the brand-management consensus collapsed. The MAGA New Right is the un-purged Bircher current, finally relieved of the optics constraints Buckley imposed.

## The Paleocon Transition

The most explicit shift from conservative to counterrevolutionary rhetoric came with [[Paleoconservatism]] in the 1980s-90s. Pat Buchanan and Sam Francis recognized that white Christian middle-class cultural dominance had eroded in ways Reagan could not reverse. The question was no longer how to slow cultural change but how to reverse it.

Sam Francis's essay on the Conservative Movement as "Beautiful Losers" — the title says it all — argued that the movement had won political victories (Reagan, tax cuts) while losing the cultural war it cared about most. Francis drew the logical conclusion: the movement needed to stop playing by the rules of a game it was losing.

Buchanan: "Our culture is superior. Our culture is superior because our religion is Christianity.... And when we are told we need to start presenting ourselves as equal to what is fundamentally far less, I say no." This is not conservation of the present; it is assertion of a supremacy the present denies.

## Counterrevolution and the New Right

The MAGA New Right has made the counterrevolutionary turn explicit in ways the older Conservative Movement kept implicit. [[Postliberalism]] theorists like [[Patrick Deneen]] argue openly for using state power to reverse liberal culture. [[The Claremonters]] speak of "counterrevolutionary moments." Glenn Ellmers wrote that the 80 million Americans who voted Biden are "not Americans in any meaningful sense of the term" — a Schmittian friend/enemy distinction applied to half the electorate.

The shift from "we are losing" to "we must win at all costs" to "those on the other side are not legitimate citizens" follows a predictable catastrophist logic. William Voegeli, senior editor of the *Claremont Review*, posed the resulting dilemma in 2022: whether it would be a bigger mistake to "keep fighting to preserve a republic that turns out to be beyond resuscitation" or to "give up defending one whose vigor might yet be restored." The question itself shows how far Claremont had moved from [[Russell Kirk]]'s assumption that the constitutional order was basically intact.

---

## Trump as the Vehicle

Joshua Tait's analysis of Trump is acute: Trump's vacuity is a feature, not a bug, of his counterrevolutionary vehicle. He "conveys a protean mix" of all the various counterrevolutionary currents — nationalism, nativism, anti-progressivism, vague Christian nationalism, nostalgia — while maintaining "sufficient deniability and authenticity simultaneously." He is an "ideological cipher who codes in so many ways he creates his own unique push/pull factors and permission structures."

This means Trump functions as a lowest-common-denominator unifier for counterrevolutionary currents that, stated explicitly, would split the coalition.
